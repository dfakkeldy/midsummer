# Chapter 2 — The players choose their parts

Quince’s house, in Athens. Quince, Snug, Bottom, Flute, Snout, and Starveling came in.

Quince asked, “Is all our company here?”

Bottom said, “You had better call them generally, man by man, according to the scrip, the written list.”

Quince said, “Here is the scroll with the name of every man in all Athens who is thought fit to act in our interlude, our little play, before the duke and the duchess, on his wedding day, at night.”

Bottom said, “First, good Peter Quince, tell us what the play is about; then read out the names of the actors; and so come to a point.”

Quince said, “Well, our play is The most lamentable comedy, and most cruel death of Pyramus and Thisby.”

Bottom said, “A very good piece of work, I assure you, and a merry one. Now, good Peter Quince, call out your actors from the scroll. Masters, spread yourselves out.”

Michael, the guide: Listen for Bottom’s slips of the tongue; he often reaches for a grand word that means the wrong thing. Asking Quince to call the men “generally” when he means one by one is the first, and the play’s own title is a muddle too: a “lamentable comedy” that promises a “most cruel death”, which Bottom then calls merry. Pyramus and Thisby are a pair of lovers from an old tale told by the Roman poet Ovid.

Quince said, “Answer as I call you. Nick Bottom, the weaver.”

Bottom said, “Ready. Name the part I am down for, and carry on.”

Quince said, “You, Nick Bottom, are set down for Pyramus.”

Bottom asked, “What is Pyramus? A lover, or a tyrant?”

Quince answered, “A lover, who kills himself most gallantly for love.”

Bottom said, “That will call for some tears, if it is performed properly. If I do it, let the audience look out for their eyes. I will raise storms; I will lament, to some extent. On to the rest. And yet my chief bent is for a tyrant. I could play Ercles splendidly, or a part to tear a cat in, a part to make everything split.”

Then Bottom gave them a sample, declaiming: “The raging rocks and shivering shocks shall break the locks of prison gates; and Phibbus’ car shall shine from far, and make and mar the foolish Fates.” And he said, “That was lofty! Now name the rest of the players. That is Ercles’ style, a tyrant’s style; a lover is more condoling.”

Michael, the guide: “Ercles” is Bottom’s way of saying Hercules, the mighty hero of ancient legend, and “a part to tear a cat in” and “to make everything split” are his phrases for the loud, ranting acting he loves; the cat image is just as odd in the original. In his verse, “shivering shocks” are shattering blows; “Phibbus” is his version of Phoebus, the sun god, whose “car”, or chariot, was pictured carrying the sun across the sky; and the Fates were the goddesses believed to control human destiny, whom the sun here will both “make and mar”, build up and ruin, which hardly makes sense, and needn’t. Bottom offers these jingling lines as proof of his ranting tyrant style; “condoling”, in his mouth, means tearful and lamenting.

Quince called, “Francis Flute, the bellows-mender.”

Flute answered, “Here, Peter Quince.”

Quince said, “Flute, you must take on Thisby.”

Flute asked, “What is Thisby? A wandering knight?”

Quince answered, “She is the lady that Pyramus must love.”

Flute said, “No, honestly, don’t make me play a woman. I have a beard coming.”

Quince said, “That makes no difference. You shall play it in a mask, and you may speak as high and small as you like.”

Bottom said, “If I can hide my face, let me play Thisby too. I’ll speak in a monstrous little voice: ‘Thisne, Thisne!’ ‘Ah, Pyramus, my lover dear! Your Thisby dear, and lady dear!’”

Quince said, “No, no. You must play Pyramus; and, Flute, you play Thisby.”

Bottom said, “Well, carry on.”

Michael, the guide: In Shakespeare’s own theatre, women’s parts were played by boys and young men, which is why a beard coming in is a real worry for Flute, and a mask Quince’s practical answer. Bottom’s “monstrous little voice” contradicts itself, and that is part of the joke. “Thisne” may be a pet form of Thisby’s name, or may mean “in this way”; editors are not certain.

Quince called, “Robin Starveling, the tailor.”

Starveling answered, “Here, Peter Quince.”

Quince said, “Robin Starveling, you must play Thisby’s mother. Tom Snout, the tinker.”

Snout answered, “Here, Peter Quince.”

Quince said, “You, Pyramus’s father. Myself, Thisby’s father. Snug, the joiner: you, the lion’s part. And there, I hope, is a play properly cast.”

Snug said, “Have you got the lion’s part written out? If you have, please give it to me, because I am slow at learning.”

Quince answered, “You can do it off the cuff, for it is nothing but roaring.”

Bottom said, “Let me play the lion too. I will roar so that it will do any man’s heart good to hear me; I will roar so that I will make the duke say, ‘Let him roar again, let him roar again!’”

Quince said, “If you did it too terrifyingly, you would frighten the duchess and the ladies so much that they would shriek; and that would be enough to get us all hanged.”

All of them agreed that it would hang them, every mother’s son of them.

Bottom said, “I grant you, friends, if you frightened the ladies out of their wits, they would have no more sense than to hang us. But I will aggravate my voice so that I will roar for you as gently as any sucking dove; I will roar for you as if it were any nightingale.”

Michael, the guide: “Aggravate” means to make something worse; Bottom means the opposite, to tone his voice down. And his promise to roar “as gently as any sucking dove” or “any nightingale” turns a lion’s roar upside down: a sucking creature is a nursing baby animal, and neither a dove nor a nightingale roars.

Quince said, “You can play no part but Pyramus; for Pyramus is a sweet-faced man; a fine, handsome man, as handsome as you would see on a summer’s day; a most lovely, gentlemanly man. So you simply must play Pyramus.”

Bottom said, “Well, I will take it on. What beard had I best play it in?”

Quince said, “Why, whatever you like.”

Bottom said, “I will carry it off in either your straw-coloured beard, your orange-tawny beard, your deep-dyed purple beard, or your French-crown-coloured beard, your perfect yellow.”

Quince said, “Some of your French crowns have no hair at all, and then you will be playing barefaced. But, masters, here are your parts. And I am to entreat you, request you, and desire you to learn them by tomorrow night, and to meet me in the palace wood, a mile outside the town, by moonlight. There we will rehearse; for if we meet in the city, we shall be dogged by people, and our plans will be known. In the meantime I will draw up a list of the props our play needs. Please, don’t fail me.”

Michael, the guide: A French crown was a gold coin, so Bottom’s “French-crown-coloured” beard is golden yellow. Quince turns the phrase into a joke about bald heads, commonly explained as a jab at the hair loss linked with the so-called French disease, syphilis; and with no hair, Bottom would play “barefaced”, meaning both beardless and openly, without disguise.

Bottom said, “We will meet; and there we can rehearse most obscenely and courageously. Take pains; be word-perfect. Farewell.”

Quince said, “We meet at the duke’s oak.”

Bottom said, “Enough. Hold, or cut bow-strings.” And they all left.

Michael, the guide: “Obscenely” is another of Bottom’s misfires; whatever he means, it is surely not what the word says. “Hold, or cut bow-strings” is an old saying whose exact sense is uncertain; it is generally taken as a pledge to keep the appointment come what may. Note the arrangements: the players will rehearse tomorrow night, by moonlight, at the duke’s oak in the palace wood, which Quince places a mile outside the town, while in the previous scene Lysander placed his meeting with Hermia, also tomorrow night, in a wood a league outside, and the play gives both figures as they stand.
