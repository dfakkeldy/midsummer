# Chapter 10 — The morning in the wood

Theseus, Hippolyta and Egeus came in with their attendants, to the place where the four young lovers still lay asleep.

Theseus said, “Go, one of you, find the forester; for now our observance is performed, and since we have the first part of the day before us, my love shall hear the music of my hounds. Unleash them in the western valley; let them go. Hurry, I say, and find the forester.” One of the attendants left. “We will go, fair queen, up to the mountain’s top, and listen to the musical confusion of the hounds and their echo sounding together.”

Hippolyta said, “I was with Hercules and Cadmus once, when in a wood in Crete they brought a bear to bay with hounds of Sparta. I never heard such splendid clamour; for besides the groves, the skies, the fountains, every region near seemed all one shared cry. I never heard so musical a discord, such sweet thunder.”

Theseus said, “My hounds are bred from the Spartan kind: they have the same heavy hanging jowls, the same sandy colour, and their heads are hung with ears that sweep away the morning dew. They are crook-kneed, with loose folds of skin at the throat like Thessalian bulls; slow in pursuit, but matched in voice like bells, each one pitched just under the next. A more tuneful pack was never hallooed to, nor cheered on with the horn, in Crete, in Sparta, or in Thessaly. Judge when you hear them. But wait! What nymphs are these?”

Michael, the guide: Hercules is the great hero of Greek legend, famous for his strength, and Cadmus is the legendary founder of the Greek city of Thebes; Crete is a Greek island, and Thessaly a region of Greece. To bring a bear to bay is to hunt it with hounds until it is cornered and held there by their barking. Theseus praises his own dogs not for speed but for their voices, matched like a peal of bells, and when he spots the sleepers he calls them “nymphs,” the maiden spirits of woods and waters in Greek myth.

Egeus said, “My lord, this is my daughter here asleep; and this is Lysander; this is Demetrius; this is Helena, old Nedar’s Helena. I wonder at their being here together.”

Theseus said, “No doubt they rose up early to observe the rite of May, and, hearing of our plans, came here to honour our celebration. But tell me, Egeus: isn’t this the day that Hermia is to give her answer about her choice?”

Egeus said, “It is, my lord.”

Theseus said, “Go, tell the huntsmen to wake them with their horns.” Out of sight, horns sounded and voices shouted, and Lysander, Demetrius, Helena and Hermia woke and started up. “Good morning, friends. Saint Valentine’s Day is past; are these woodbirds only now beginning to pair off?”

Michael, the guide: The “observance” Theseus mentioned is this rite of May: by old custom people went out at dawn on May morning to celebrate, though the play’s own title speaks of midsummer. Birds were said to choose their mates on Saint Valentine’s Day, so Theseus teases the waking young people as birds late to couple. And notice his question about the day: at the very start he gave Hermia until the new moon, four days off, to make her choice, yet the lovers set out the following night and we have seen only one night in the wood. The play leaves its timing just as it is.

Lysander said, “Pardon, my lord.”

Theseus said, “Please, all of you, stand up. I know you two are rival enemies. How has this gentle harmony come into the world, so that hatred is so far from suspicion that it sleeps beside the one it hates and fears no hostility?”

Lysander said, “My lord, I shall answer in bewilderment, half asleep, half awake. But so far, I swear, I cannot truly say how I came here. But as I think — for I want to speak the truth, and now that I come to think of it, this is how it is — I came here with Hermia. Our intention was to get away from Athens, to somewhere we might be beyond the danger of the Athenian law.”

Egeus said, “Enough, enough, my lord; you have heard enough. I ask for the law, the law, to come down upon his head. They would have stolen away; they would have, Demetrius, and so defeated you and me: cheated you of your wife, and made nothing of my consent, my consent that she should be your wife.”

Demetrius said, “My lord, fair Helen told me of their secret flight, of this plan of theirs to come here to this wood; and I, in fury, followed them here, with fair Helena following me out of love. But, my good lord, I don’t know by what power — but it is some power — my love for Hermia has melted like the snow, and seems to me now like the memory of some worthless trinket that I doted on in my childhood. And all the faith, the devotion of my heart, the object and the pleasure of my eye, is only Helena. To her, my lord, I was betrothed before I ever saw Hermia. But, like someone in sickness, I loathed this food; now, as in health, I have come back to my natural taste, and now I wish for it, love it, long for it, and will be true to it for evermore.”

Theseus said, “Fair lovers, you have met with good fortune. We will hear more of this story shortly. Egeus, I will overrule your will; for in the temple, very soon, along with us, these couples shall be joined together for ever. And since the morning is now somewhat worn away, our planned hunting shall be set aside. Away with us to Athens! Three and three, we’ll hold a feast in great ceremony. Come, Hippolyta.” Theseus, Hippolyta, Egeus and their attendants left.

Michael, the guide: Demetrius cannot name the power that changed him; we saw that it was the love-juice, and unlike Lysander he has never been given the remedy. His claim that he was betrothed to Helena before he saw Hermia is his own statement; at the start, Lysander said only that Demetrius had courted her. And Theseus, who at the start told Hermia that the law of Athens was beyond his power to soften, now simply overrules her father; Egeus says nothing more before he goes. “Three and three” seems to mean three couples, the Duke and Hippolyta among them, though Theseus never says aloud who is to marry whom.

Demetrius said, “These things seem small and impossible to make out, like far-off mountains turned into clouds.”

Hermia said, “I think I see these things with my eyes out of focus, when everything seems double.”

Helena said, “So do I. And I have found Demetrius like a jewel: my own, and not my own.”

Michael, the guide: Hermia means her eyes seem to be looking separately, so everything appears twice. Helena compares Demetrius to a jewel someone has found: hers because she has it in her hand, yet perhaps not truly hers to keep.

Demetrius said, “Are you sure that we are awake? It seems to me that we are still asleep, still dreaming. Don’t you think the Duke was here, and told us to follow him?”

Hermia said, “Yes; and my father.”

Helena said, “And Hippolyta.”

Lysander said, “And he did tell us to follow him to the temple.”

Demetrius said, “Why, then, we are awake. Let’s follow him, and on the way let’s tell each other our dreams.” And the four of them left.

Bottom woke and said, “When my cue comes, call me, and I will answer. My next is, ‘Most fair Pyramus.’ Heigh-ho! Peter Quince! Flute, the bellows-mender! Snout, the tinker! Starveling! God’s my life! They’ve stolen off and left me asleep! I have had a most rare vision. I have had a dream, past the wit of man to say what dream it was. A man is nothing but an ass if he goes about explaining this dream. I thought I was — there is no man can tell what. I thought I was — and I thought I had — but a man is nothing but a patched fool if he will offer to say what I thought I had. The eye of man has not heard, the ear of man has not seen, man’s hand is not able to taste, his tongue to conceive, nor his heart to report, what my dream was. I will get Peter Quince to write a ballad of this dream. It shall be called ‘Bottom’s Dream,’ because it has no bottom; and I will sing it at the end of a play, before the Duke. Perhaps, to make it all the more gracious, I shall sing it at her death.” And he left.

Michael, the guide: Bottom wakes as if still at the rehearsal, waiting for his cue. He breaks off each time he tries to say what he thought he was, or had, and calls any man who explains the dream an ass, the very animal whose head he wore. His jumble of the senses, the eye hearing and the ear seeing, scrambles a well-known Bible passage from Saint Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians about what eye has not seen nor ear heard. A “patched fool” is a jester in a patchwork costume, and a dream with “no bottom” is a pun on his name: too deep to measure. Whose death he means by “her death,” he doesn’t say.
