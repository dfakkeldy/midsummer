# Chapter 1 — The marriage dispute

The palace of Theseus, Duke of Athens. Theseus came in with Hippolyta, the woman he was soon to marry, together with Philostrate, a member of his court, and attendants.

Theseus said, “Now, fair Hippolyta, the hour of our wedding is coming on fast. Four happy days will bring in a new moon. But oh, how slowly this old moon seems to me to wane! She keeps my desires waiting, like a stepmother, or like a widow with a life-share in an estate, who goes on for years slowly draining away the income a young heir is waiting to inherit.”

Hippolyta answered, “Four days will quickly soak themselves in night; four nights will quickly dream the time away. And then the moon, like a silver bow newly bent in the sky, will look down on the night of our wedding ceremonies.”

Theseus gave an order. “Go, Philostrate. Stir up the young people of Athens to celebrate. Wake the lively, quick-footed spirit of fun. Send sadness off to funerals, where it belongs; that pale companion has no place in our festivities.” Philostrate left.

Then Theseus spoke again to Hippolyta. “Hippolyta, I courted you with my sword, and won your love by doing you injuries. But I will marry you in a different key: with pageantry, with public celebration, and with revelling.”

Michael, the guide: Two things to hold on to from this opening. First, the wedding of Theseus and Hippolyta is set for the night of the new moon, four days away. Second, Theseus himself says the courtship began in violence: in his words, he won her with his sword and by doing her injuries. The scene does not tell us how Hippolyta regards that, and her few words leave room for more than one hearing. “Another key” is a musical image: the same match, he says, will now be played in a festive tone.

Egeus came in with his daughter Hermia and two young men, Lysander and Demetrius.

Egeus said, “Happiness to Theseus, our famous duke!”

Theseus replied, “Thank you, good Egeus. What news do you bring?”

Egeus said, “I come full of distress and anger, with a complaint against my own child, my daughter Hermia. Step forward, Demetrius. My noble lord, this man has my permission to marry her. Step forward, Lysander. And, my gracious duke, this man has bewitched my child’s heart. You, yes you, Lysander, you have given her poems and exchanged love-tokens with her. You have sung at her window by moonlight, in a voice pretending love, verses pretending love. You have stealthily stamped yourself on her imagination, the way a seal is pressed into wax, with bracelets woven from your hair, with rings, trinkets, clever little love-gifts, knick-knacks, trifles, posies of flowers, and sweets: messengers with great power over young people who have not yet learned to resist them.”

Egeus went on. “With cunning you have stolen my daughter’s heart, and turned the obedience she owes to me into stubborn harshness. And so, my gracious duke, if she will not agree here, before your Grace, to marry Demetrius, I ask for the ancient right of Athens: since she is mine, I may dispose of her as I choose. And that will be either to this gentleman, Demetrius, or to her death, according to our law, which speaks directly to such a case.”

Theseus turned the question to Hermia. “What do you say, Hermia? Think carefully, lovely girl. Your father ought to be like a god to you: the one who made your beauty; yes, and one to whom you are no more than a shape pressed into wax, imprinted by him, so that it is in his power either to leave the figure as it is or to deface it. Demetrius is a worthy gentleman.”

Hermia said, “So is Lysander.”

Theseus answered, “In himself, he is. But in this matter, since he lacks your father’s vote, the other must be considered the worthier.”

Hermia said, “I wish my father could see only with my eyes.”

Theseus said, “Rather, your eyes must see with his judgement.”

Hermia said, “I beg your Grace to pardon me. I do not know what power makes me so bold, or how it may reflect on my modesty to speak my mind in such company. But I beg your Grace to let me know the worst that may happen to me in this case, if I refuse to marry Demetrius.”

Theseus told her. “Either to be put to death, or to give up for ever the company of men. So, fair Hermia, question your desires. Consider how young you are; examine your passions closely; ask yourself whether, if you do not yield to your father’s choice, you can bear to wear the habit of a nun: to be shut away for ever in a shadowy cloister, living all your life as a childless sister, chanting faint hymns to the cold, barren moon. Those who can master their passions enough to make that lifelong maiden pilgrimage are three times blessed. But in earthly terms, a rose that is picked and distilled into perfume is happier than one that withers on its untouched stem, and grows, lives, and dies in single blessedness.”

Hermia answered, “So will I grow, so live, so die, my lord, before I surrender my maidenhood, my right to it, like a charter granted to me, to his lordship, whose unwanted yoke my soul refuses to accept as its master.”

Theseus said, “Take time to think. And by the next new moon, the day when my love and I seal the everlasting bond of our partnership, on that day be ready either to die for disobeying your father’s will, or else to marry Demetrius, as he would have it, or to vow at the altar of Diana a life of austerity and singleness for ever.”

Michael, the guide: Hermia now faces three possible outcomes, not two. Egeus asked for marriage or death; Theseus adds a third path, a vow at the altar of Diana, goddess of the moon and of chastity, to live unmarried for life, which he pictures as a nun’s life in a cloister, using words from Shakespeare’s own world for an ancient Athenian setting. Egeus calls this law an ancient privilege of Athens; the play presents it as the law of its own Athens, not as a record of real history. Theseus’s rose image is one way of saying that married life, like a rose turned into lasting perfume, is happier on earth than a life that fades untouched. Hermia’s deadline is the new moon: the duke’s own wedding day, four days off.

Demetrius said, “Give way, sweet Hermia. And, Lysander, give up your cracked, flawed claim to my certain right.”

Lysander replied, “You have her father’s love, Demetrius. Let me have Hermia’s. You go and marry him.”

Egeus said, “Scornful Lysander! True, he has my love, and my love will give him what is mine. And she is mine, and all my rights in her I legally hand over to Demetrius.”

Lysander addressed the duke. “My lord, I come from as good a family as he does, and I am as wealthy. My love is greater than his. My prospects are in every way as fair as Demetrius’s, if not better. And, what counts for more than all these boasts, I am loved by beautiful Hermia. So why shouldn’t I pursue my claim? Demetrius, and I will say it to his face, courted Nedar’s daughter, Helena, and won her heart. And she, sweet lady, dotes, devoutly dotes, dotes to the point of worship, on this stained and fickle man.”

Theseus said, “I must admit I have heard as much, and meant to speak to Demetrius about it; but, being overloaded with my own affairs, I let it slip my mind. But come, Demetrius; and come, Egeus. You shall go with me; I have some private advice for you both. As for you, fair Hermia, see that you prepare yourself to fit your fancies to your father’s will. Otherwise the law of Athens, which I can in no way soften, hands you over to death, or to a vow of single life.”

Theseus went on. “Come, my Hippolyta. How are you, my love? Demetrius and Egeus, come along. I need you for some business for our wedding, and I want to talk with you about something that closely concerns yourselves.”

Egeus replied, “We follow you out of duty, and gladly.” Everyone left except Lysander and Hermia.

Lysander said, “What is it, my love? Why is your cheek so pale? How is it that the roses there are fading so fast?”

Hermia answered, “Perhaps for lack of rain, which I could easily give them from the storm in my eyes.”

Lysander said, “Oh dear! From everything I have ever read, or ever heard in story or history, the course of true love never did run smooth. Either the lovers were of different rank by birth—”

Hermia broke in, “What a burden! To be too high-born to be bound to someone low.”

Lysander went on, “Or else they were badly grafted together, like mismatched branches, in their ages—”

Hermia said, “How cruel! To be too old to be pledged to someone young.”

Lysander continued, “Or else it depended on the choice of family and friends—”

Hermia said, “What hell, to choose love through someone else’s eyes!”

Lysander said, “Or, if the two did choose each other, war, death, or sickness laid siege to their love, making it as momentary as a sound, as swift as a shadow, as short as any dream, as brief as lightning in the coal-black night, which in one sudden flash reveals both heaven and earth, and before a man can say ‘Look!’ the jaws of darkness swallow it up. That is how quickly bright things come to ruin.”

Hermia answered, “If true lovers have always been thwarted, then it stands as a decree of destiny. So let us teach ourselves patience in our trial, since it is a usual burden, as much a part of love as thoughts and dreams and sighs, wishes and tears, the poor attendants that follow love’s imagination.”

Lysander said, “A good way to think of it. So listen to me, Hermia. I have an aunt, a widow, with a large income of her own, and she has no children. Her house is seven leagues away from Athens, and she regards me as her only son. There, gentle Hermia, I can marry you, and the harsh law of Athens cannot follow us there. If you love me, then, slip out of your father’s house tomorrow night, and in the wood just one league outside the town, where I once met you with Helena when you were celebrating the rites of a May morning, there I will wait for you.”

Hermia answered, “My good Lysander! I swear to you by Cupid’s strongest bow, by his best arrow with the golden head, by the innocence of Venus’s doves, by whatever binds souls together and makes love prosper, by the fire that burned the queen of Carthage when she saw the faithless Trojan sailing away, and by all the vows that men have ever broken, more of them than women have ever spoken: in that very place you have named, tomorrow I will truly meet you.”

Michael, the guide: Hermia’s oath piles up images from ancient stories. Cupid, the god of love, was said to shoot arrows, and the golden-tipped one was believed to kindle love. Venus, goddess of love, was pictured with doves. The “queen of Carthage” is Dido, and the “faithless Trojan” is Aeneas, who sailed away and abandoned her. Hermia’s line links the fire that burned the queen to that moment of desertion, so she is swearing by the best-known example of a lover betrayed. One way to hear the oath is as both sincere and teasing: Hermia swears by men’s broken promises, and adds a jab that men have broken more vows than women have ever spoken.

Lysander said, “Keep your promise, love. Look, here comes Helena.” Helena came in.

Hermia called, “God speed you, fair Helena! Where are you going?”

Helena replied, “Do you call me fair? Take that ‘fair’ back. It is your fairness Demetrius loves. Oh, lucky fairness! Your eyes are guiding stars, like the pole star sailors steer by, and the sweet music of your voice is more tuneful than a lark to a shepherd’s ear in spring, when the wheat is green and the hawthorn buds appear. Sickness is catching. Oh, if only good looks were catching too, I would catch yours, fair Hermia, before I go. My ear would catch your voice, my eye your eye, my tongue the sweet melody of your tongue. If the whole world were mine, I would give it all, except Demetrius, to be transformed into you. Oh, teach me how you look, and by what art you steer the movements of Demetrius’s heart!”

Hermia said, “I frown at him, yet he still loves me.”

Helena said, “Oh, if only your frowns could teach my smiles such skill!”

Hermia said, “I give him curses, yet he gives me love.”

Helena said, “Oh, if only my prayers could stir such affection!”

Hermia said, “The more I hate him, the more he follows me.”

Helena said, “The more I love him, the more he hates me.”

Hermia said, “His foolishness, Helena, is no fault of mine.”

Helena said, “No fault but your beauty. I wish that fault were mine!”

Hermia said, “Take comfort. He will never see my face again. Lysander and I are going to run away from this place. Before I ever saw Lysander, Athens seemed like paradise to me. Oh, then, what powers must live in my love, that he has turned a heaven into a hell!”

Lysander said, “Helen, we will tell you our plans. Tomorrow night, when Phoebe, the moon, sees her silver face in the mirror of the water, and decorates the blades of grass with liquid pearls of dew, at the hour that always hides lovers’ escapes, we have planned to slip out through the gates of Athens.”

Hermia added, “And in the wood, where you and I often used to lie on beds of pale primroses, pouring out to each other the sweet secrets of our hearts, there Lysander and I will meet; and from there we will turn our eyes away from Athens, to look for new friends and the company of strangers. Goodbye, sweet playmate. Pray for us, and may good luck give you your Demetrius! Keep your word, Lysander. We must starve our eyes of the sight of each other, which is lovers’ food, until deep midnight tomorrow.”

Lysander answered, “I will, my Hermia.” Hermia left.

Lysander said, “Helena, goodbye. May Demetrius dote on you as you dote on him!” He left, and Helena was alone.

Helena said, “How much happier some people can be than others! All through Athens I am thought to be as beautiful as she is. But what good is that? Demetrius doesn’t think so. He refuses to see what everyone but him can see. And just as he goes wrong in doting on Hermia’s eyes, so do I in admiring his qualities. Love can take things that are low and vile, things of no worth that count for nothing, and transform them into something with shape and dignity. Love does not look with the eyes but with the mind, and that is why winged Cupid is painted blind. Nor does Love’s mind have the least taste for judgement: wings and no eyes stand for reckless haste. And that is why Love is said to be a child, because he is so often fooled in his choices.”

Helena went on. “Just as playful boys break their word in their games, so the boy Love breaks his oaths everywhere. For before Demetrius looked into Hermia’s eyes, he showered down oaths like hail that he was mine alone; and when this hail felt some heat from Hermia, he melted, and his showers of oaths dissolved. I will go and tell him about fair Hermia’s escape. Then tomorrow night he will follow her to the wood. And if I get any thanks for this news, it will be dearly bought. But by doing this I mean to make my pain richer: to have the sight of him on the way there and back again.” She left.

Michael, the guide: Helena has just described love as blind, hasty, and easily fooled, and she counts herself among its fools; yet she decides to act on her love anyway, by telling Demetrius her friend’s secret. One way to hear her last lines is that she expects the plan to hurt her and chooses it for the chance to see him, there and back. The timing and places to hold on to: Hermia and Lysander mean to meet tomorrow night in the wood, one league outside Athens, and go on from there to the aunt’s house, which Lysander places seven leagues from Athens, and where, he says, Athenian law cannot follow. A league is an old measure of distance; what matters here is that the wood lies close to the town, while the aunt’s house lies much farther from Athens. The duke’s deadline for Hermia is the new moon, his own wedding day.
