# Chapter 4 — The wrong man

In another part of the wood, Titania came in with her train of attendants.

Titania said, “Come, now a roundel, a dance in a ring, and a fairy song; then, for the third part of a minute, off you go: some to kill the canker grubs in the musk-rose buds; some to wage war on the bats, the rere-mice, for their leathery wings, to make coats for my small elves; and some to keep back the noisy owl that hoots every night and marvels at our dainty spirits. Now sing me to sleep; then go about your duties, and let me rest.”

One fairy sang:
“You spotted snakes with forked tongue,
Thorny hedgehogs, be not seen;
Newts and blind-worms, do no wrong,
Come not near our fairy queen.”
Then came the chorus:
“Philomel, with melody,
Sing along in our sweet lullaby;
Lulla, lulla, lullaby, lulla, lulla, lullaby:
Never harm,
Nor spell, nor charm,
Come near our lovely lady;
So, good night, with lullaby.”

The fairy sang on:
“Weaving spiders, come not here;
Off with you, long-legged spinners, off!
Black beetles, come not near;
Worm and snail, do no offence.”
And the chorus came round again:
“Philomel, with melody,
Sing along in our sweet lullaby;
Lulla, lulla, lullaby, lulla, lulla, lullaby:
Never harm,
Nor spell, nor charm,
Come near our lovely lady;
So, good night, with lullaby.”

Michael, the guide: Philomel is a poetic name for the nightingale, taken from Philomela, a woman in classical myth who was turned into a bird; the fairies are calling on the nightingale to sing along with them. Blind-worms are slow-worms, small legless lizards, and the song treats them, like the newts, as creatures that might do the queen harm.

Another fairy said, “Away now! All is well. Let one of us stand apart and keep watch.” Then the fairies went away, and Titania slept.

Oberon came in and squeezed the flower on Titania’s eyelids, saying, “Whatever you see when you wake, take that for your true love; love and pine for his sake. Be it ounce, or cat, or bear, pard, or boar with bristling hair, whatever appears in your eye when you wake, it is your dear. Wake when some vile thing is near.” And he left.

Michael, the guide: An ounce was an old name for a lynx or a similar wild cat, and a pard is a leopard. Oberon’s spell ends with a wish that Titania will wake when some vile creature is close by.

Lysander and Hermia came in. Lysander said, “My lovely love, you are faint from wandering in the wood; and to tell the truth, I have lost our way. We’ll rest, Hermia, if you think it good, and wait for the comfort of daylight.”

Hermia said, “So be it, Lysander. Find yourself a bed, for I will rest my head upon this bank.”

Lysander said, “One piece of turf shall serve as a pillow for us both: one heart, one bed, two bosoms, and one pledge of faith.”

Hermia said, “No, good Lysander. For my sake, my dear, lie further off still; do not lie so near.”

Lysander said, “Oh, sweet, take my meaning as the innocent thing it is! When lovers talk together, love should understand love’s meaning. I mean that my heart is knit to yours, so that we can make only one heart of the two: two bosoms chained together by an oath, and so two bosoms and a single pledge. Then don’t deny me room to sleep by your side; for lying so, Hermia, I do not lie.”

Michael, the guide: Lysander is playing on two senses of “lie”: lying down beside her, and telling a lie, so he is promising that his intentions are honest.

Hermia said, “Lysander makes very pretty riddles. Curse my manners and my pride if Hermia meant to say that Lysander lied. But, gentle friend, for love and courtesy, lie further off. In human modesty, keep the kind of distance that may well be called proper between a virtuous bachelor and a maiden; stay that far away. And good night, sweet friend. May your love never change until your sweet life ends!”

Lysander said, “Amen, amen, to that fair prayer, I say; and may my life end when my loyalty ends! Here is my bed. May sleep give you all his rest!”

Hermia said, “And may half that wish close the wisher’s own eyes!” And they slept.

Puck came in and said, “I have gone through the forest, but found no Athenian on whose eyes I might test this flower’s power to stir up love. Night and silence. Who is here? He is wearing Athenian clothes. This is the one my master said despised the Athenian maid; and here is the maiden, sleeping soundly on the damp and dirty ground. Pretty soul! She didn’t dare lie near this lack-love, this kill-courtesy. You boor, upon your eyes I throw all the power this charm possesses. When you wake, let love keep sleep from taking its seat on your eyelid. So wake when I am gone; for I must go now to Oberon.” And he left.

Michael, the guide: Oberon gave Puck only one way to recognise the man, his Athenian clothes, and Lysander is wearing them too; so Puck has charmed Lysander, not the young man Oberon overheard scorning Helena. Puck also reads the distance between the sleepers as proof that the man is cold to her, though we heard that it was Hermia who asked Lysander to lie further off.

Demetrius and Helena came in, running. Helena said, “Stop, even if you kill me, sweet Demetrius.”

Demetrius said, “I order you, go away, and don’t haunt me like this.”

Helena said, “Oh, will you leave me here in the dark? Don’t do it.”

Demetrius said, “Stay here, at your peril. I will go alone.” And he left.

Helena said, “Oh, I am out of breath in this foolish chase! The more I pray, the less favour I get. Hermia is happy, wherever she is lying, for she has blessed eyes that draw people to her. How did her eyes become so bright? Not with salt tears; if that were it, my eyes are washed more often than hers. No, no, I am as ugly as a bear, for beasts that meet me run away in fear. So it is no wonder that Demetrius runs from my presence as if from a monster. What wicked, lying mirror of mine made me think I could compare with Hermia’s eyes, bright as the stars in their spheres?”

Helena said, “But who is here? Lysander! On the ground! Dead, or asleep? I see no blood, no wound. Lysander, if you are alive, good sir, wake up.”

Lysander woke and said, “And I will run through fire for your sweet sake! Transparent Helena! Nature shows her art here, letting me see your heart through your breast. Where is Demetrius? Oh, how fit that vile name is to perish on my sword!”

Helena said, “Don’t say so, Lysander; don’t say so. What if he does love your Hermia? Lord, what of it? Hermia still loves you, so be content.”

Lysander said, “Content with Hermia! No. I regret the tedious minutes I have spent with her. It is not Hermia but Helena I love. Who would not exchange a raven for a dove? A man’s will is ruled by his reason, and reason says you are the worthier maiden. Things that grow are not ripe until their season; so I, being young, was not ripe for reason until now. And now that I have reached the height of human judgment, reason becomes the marshal of my will, and leads me to your eyes, where I read love’s stories, written in love’s richest book.”

Michael, the guide: A raven is black and a dove white, so Lysander is trading what he now calls the lesser bird for the finer one, with Hermia as the raven. He insists that reason has changed his mind; but we have just heard Puck lay the flower’s charm on his eyes, and Helena was the first thing he saw when he woke.

Helena said, “Why was I born to suffer this cutting mockery? When did I ever deserve such scorn from you? Isn’t it enough, isn’t it enough, young man, that I never did, no, and never can, deserve one sweet look from Demetrius’ eye, without you mocking my shortcomings too? Truly, you do me wrong, truly you do, to woo me in such a scornful way. But farewell. I have to confess I thought you had more true gentleness in you, more of a true gentleman’s courtesy. Oh, that a lady refused by one man should, for that very reason, be abused by another!” And she left.

Lysander said, “She doesn’t see Hermia. Hermia, sleep there, and may you never come near Lysander! For just as too much of the sweetest things brings the deepest loathing to the stomach, or as the heresies men give up, the false religious teachings that once misled them, are hated most by the very people they once deceived, so you, my surfeit and my heresy, be hated by everyone, and most of all by me! And all my powers, turn your love and strength to honouring Helen and being her knight!” And he left.

Hermia woke and cried, “Help me, Lysander, help me! Do your best to pluck this crawling serpent off my breast! Oh, have pity! What a dream that was! Lysander, look how I am shaking with fear. I thought a serpent was eating my heart away, and you sat smiling as it cruelly preyed on me.”

Hermia said, “Lysander! What, have you moved away? Lysander! Lord! What, out of hearing? Gone? No sound, no word? Alas, where are you? Speak, if you can hear me; speak, for love’s sake! I am almost fainting with fear. No? Then I can see well enough that you are not near. I’ll find either death or you, at once.” And she left.
