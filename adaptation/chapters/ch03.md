# Chapter 3 — The quarrel in the fairy kingdom

A wood near Athens. A fairy came in from one side, and Puck from the other.

Puck said, “Hello there, spirit! Where are you wandering?”

The fairy answered, “Over hill, over valley, through bush, through briar, over park, over fence, through flood, through fire, I wander everywhere, swifter than the moon’s turning sphere. And I serve the fairy queen, to sprinkle dew on her circles on the green. The tall cowslips are her bodyguards: on their gold coats you see spots; those are rubies, fairy gifts, and in those freckles lives their sweet scent. I must go and look for some dewdrops here, and hang a pearl in every cowslip’s ear. Farewell, you lob, you lout among spirits; I’ll be gone. Our queen and all her elves are coming here soon.”

Michael, the guide: The fairy’s circles, or “orbs”, are fairy rings, the darker rings in grass that country people said fairies made by dancing. The “pensioners” were the tall gentlemen who served as the English monarch’s ceremonial bodyguard, so the tall cowslips are the fairy queen’s guard. And “lob” is a teasing insult, something like lout or bumpkin.

Puck said, “The king is holding his revels here tonight. Take care that the queen does not come within his sight, for Oberon is fiercely angry and full of wrath, because she has as her attendant a lovely boy, stolen from an Indian king; she never had so sweet a changeling. And jealous Oberon wants the child as a knight in his train, to range through the wild forests; but she withholds the beloved boy by force, crowns him with flowers, and makes him all her joy. And now they never meet in grove or on green, by a clear spring, or in the shine of spangled starlight, without squaring up to each other, so that all their elves creep into acorn cups for fear and hide there.”

Michael, the guide: In folklore, a changeling usually means the fairy child left in place of a stolen human baby; here the word is used for the stolen human boy himself. Bear in mind that this is Puck’s account of where the boy came from. “Squaring up” keeps Shakespeare’s word “square”, meaning to quarrel and face off.

The fairy said, “Either I quite mistake your shape and build, or else you are that shrewd, mischievous sprite called Robin Goodfellow. Aren’t you the one who frightens the village girls; skims the cream off the milk; sometimes toils at the hand mill; makes the breathless housewife churn and churn for nothing; sometimes stops the ale from rising with its froth; and leads night wanderers astray, laughing at their harm? Those who call you Hobgoblin, and sweet Puck, you do their work for them, and they shall have good luck. Aren’t you he?”

Michael, the guide: Robin Goodfellow was a mischievous household spirit of English country folklore, and “hobgoblin” and “puck” were names for spirits of that kind, so Puck’s name is almost a description. In the fairy’s list, the hand mill is a quern, a small stone mill for grinding grain, and the missing froth is barm, the yeasty head that forms on fermenting ale.

Puck said, “You are right; I am that merry wanderer of the night. I make jokes for Oberon and make him smile when I trick a fat, bean-fed horse by neighing like a young filly. And sometimes I lurk in an old gossip’s drinking bowl, looking exactly like a roasted crab apple, and when she drinks I bob against her lips and pour the ale down her withered dewlap, the loose skin of her throat. The wisest old aunt, telling the most solemn, serious tale, sometimes mistakes me for a three-legged stool; then I slip out from under her bum, down she topples, and cries ‘Tailor!’, and falls into a fit of coughing; and then the whole company hold their hips and laugh, and their mirth grows, and they sneeze, and swear that a merrier hour was never wasted there. But make room, fairy! Here comes Oberon.”

The fairy said, “And here comes my mistress. I wish he were gone!”

Michael, the guide: A “gossip” here is an old woman among her friends, and an “aunt” is any old woman, not necessarily a relative; roasted crab apples were floated in hot ale, which is the disguise Puck takes. Why the fallen woman cries “Tailor!” is uncertain; one common guess connects it with landing on her backside, her “tail”.

Oberon came in from one side with his followers, and Titania from the other with hers.

Oberon said, “This is an ill meeting by moonlight, proud Titania.”

Titania said, “What, jealous Oberon! Fairies, skip away from here. I have sworn off his bed and his company.”

Oberon said, “Wait, you rash wanton! Am I not your lord and husband?”

Titania said, “Then I must be your lady. But I know of the times you have stolen away from fairyland, and in the shape of the shepherd Corin sat all day playing on pipes made from stalks of corn, and making love-verses to the amorous Phillida. Why are you here, come from the farthest steppe of India? Only because, of course, that bouncing Amazon, your mistress in hunting boots, your warrior love, must be married to Theseus, and you have come to give their bed joy and prosperity.”

Michael, the guide: Oberon’s word “wanton” calls Titania unruly and self-willed, with a suggestion of sexual looseness; it is his insult, not the play’s verdict. Corin and Phillida are traditional names for a shepherd and shepherdess in pastoral love poetry, so Titania is accusing Oberon of courting a shepherdess while disguised as a shepherd. The “bouncing Amazon” is Hippolyta, the warrior queen Theseus is about to marry; Shakespeare calls her “buskined”, wearing the high boots called buskins. Titania claims Hippolyta was Oberon’s mistress, and Oberon answers with a charge of his own.

Oberon said, “How can you, for shame, Titania, take sly aim at my good name with Hippolyta, knowing that I know about your love for Theseus? Didn’t you lead him through the glimmering night away from Perigenia, whom he had ravished? And make him, with the fair Aegle, break his faith, with Ariadne and Antiopa?”

Michael, the guide: Oberon’s names come from old legends of Theseus and the women in them; the best known is Ariadne, the princess of Crete who helped him escape the Labyrinth and whom he then deserted. The line about Aegle can be read two ways: that Theseus, taken up with Aegle, broke faith with Ariadne and Antiopa, or that Aegle was one more woman whose trust he broke. “Ravished” can mean carried off by force, or raped, and the line does not settle which. These are Oberon’s charges, and Titania calls them jealous inventions.

Titania said, “These are the forgeries of jealousy, lies made up by your jealousy. And never, since the beginning of midsummer, have we met on hill, in valley, forest, or meadow, by a stone-paved fountain or a reedy brook, or on the beach at the margin of the sea, to dance our ring-dances to the whistling wind, without your brawling disturbing our sport. So the winds, having piped music to us for nothing, have taken revenge, as it were, and sucked up infectious fogs from the sea; and these, falling on the land, have made every paltry river so swollen with pride that they have burst over their banks. So the ox has strained at his yoke for nothing, the ploughman has wasted his sweat, and the green corn has rotted before its youth could grow a beard. The sheepfold stands empty in the drowned field, and crows grow fat on the flock that died of disease. The nine men’s morris board is filled up with mud, and the intricate mazes in the lush grass can no longer be made out, because no one treads them.”

Titania went on, “The human mortals lack their winter here; no night now is blessed with hymn or carol. So the moon, who governs the waters, pale with anger, soaks all the air, so that rheumatic illnesses, colds and chills, abound. And through this disorder we see the seasons change: white-haired frosts fall into the fresh lap of the crimson rose, and on old Hiems’ thin and icy crown a sweet-smelling garland of summer buds is set, as if in mockery. Spring, summer, fruitful autumn, angry winter, all change their usual clothing, and the bewildered world, judging by what each one produces, cannot now tell which is which. And this whole brood of evils comes from our quarrel, from our disagreement; we are their parents and their origin.”

Michael, the guide: Hiems is Latin for winter, pictured as an old man with a crown of ice. Nine men’s morris was a game played on lines cut into turf, and the mazes were winding paths kept clear in the grass by people treading them. The line about mortals lacking “their winter here” puzzles editors; it may mean they lack a proper winter, or winter’s festive cheer, and the next line about missing hymns and carols leaves both readings open.

Oberon said, “Then you put it right; it lies in your power. Why should Titania cross her Oberon? I only beg for a little changeling boy, to be my henchman, my page.”

Titania said, “Set your heart at rest: all fairyland could not buy the child from me. His mother was a votaress, a sworn follower, of my order; and in the spice-scented Indian air, at night, she very often gossiped by my side, and sat with me on Neptune’s yellow sands, watching the merchant ships out on the tide, when we laughed to see the sails conceive and grow big-bellied with the playful wind. And she, with a pretty, gliding, swimming walk, would follow and imitate them, her womb then rich with my young squire, and sail across the land to fetch me trinkets, and come back again, as if from a voyage, laden with merchandise. But she, being mortal, died of bearing that boy; and for her sake I am bringing up her boy; and for her sake I will not part with him.”

Michael, the guide: Neptune was the Roman god of the sea, so his yellow sands are the seashore. Notice that Puck said the boy was stolen from an Indian king, while Titania speaks only of the boy’s mortal mother, her devoted follower; the play does not reconcile the two accounts.

Oberon asked, “How long do you intend to stay in this wood?”

Titania said, “Perhaps until after Theseus’ wedding day. If you will dance patiently in our circle and watch our moonlight revels, come with us; if not, avoid me, and I will keep away from your haunts.”

Oberon said, “Give me that boy, and I will go with you.”

Titania said, “Not for your whole fairy kingdom. Fairies, away! We shall quarrel outright if I stay any longer.” Then Titania left with her followers.

Oberon said, “Well, go your way. You shall not leave this grove until I torment you for this injury. My gentle Puck, come here. You remember the time when I sat on a headland and heard a mermaid on a dolphin’s back give out such sweet and harmonious sound that the rough sea grew gentle at her song, and certain stars shot madly out of their spheres to hear the sea-maid’s music?”

Puck said, “I remember.”

Oberon said, “At that very time I saw, though you could not, Cupid, fully armed, flying between the cold moon and the earth. He took sure aim at a fair virgin enthroned in the west, and let fly his love arrow smartly from his bow, as if it would pierce a hundred thousand hearts. But I could see young Cupid’s fiery arrow quenched in the chaste beams of the watery moon, and the royal votaress passed on, deep in maiden thought, free of love’s fancy. Yet I marked where Cupid’s arrow fell. It fell on a little western flower, once milk-white, now purple with love’s wound, and girls call it love-in-idleness. Fetch me that flower; it is the herb I showed you once. Its juice, laid on sleeping eyelids, will make either man or woman madly dote on the next living creature that the eye sees. Fetch me this herb, and be back here before the leviathan can swim a league.”

Michael, the guide: Cupid is the Roman god of love, whose arrows make their targets fall in love; the moon belonged to Diana, goddess of the moon and of chastity, which is why its chaste beams put the arrow out. The “fair vestal” and “imperial votaress” who passes untouched are commonly read as a compliment to Queen Elizabeth the First, England’s unmarried queen when the play was written, though the text never names her. Love-in-idleness is an old country name for the wild pansy, and the leviathan is a vast sea creature from the Bible.

Puck said, “I’ll put a belt round the whole earth in forty minutes.” And he left.

Oberon said, “Once I have this juice, I’ll watch Titania when she is asleep and drop the liquid into her eyes. Then the next thing she looks at when she wakes, whether it is a lion, a bear, a wolf, or a bull, a meddling monkey, or a busy ape, she shall pursue it with the soul of love. And before I take this spell off her sight, as I can with another herb, I’ll make her surrender her page to me. But who is coming here? I am invisible, and I will overhear their conversation.”

Demetrius came in, with Helena following him.

Demetrius said, “I don’t love you, so stop pursuing me. Where are Lysander and fair Hermia? The one I’ll kill; the other is killing me. You told me they had stolen away into this wood; and here I am, and wode, gone mad, within this wood, because I cannot find my Hermia. Away, get out, and follow me no more.”

Helena said, “You draw me to you, you hard-hearted adamant; but it isn’t iron you draw, for my heart is as true as steel. Give up your power to draw, and I shall have no power to follow you.”

Michael, the guide: “Wode” is an old word for mad, so Demetrius is mad within this wood, a pun on two words that sounded alike. Adamant was a legendary stone thought to be both the hardest of all substances and a magnet; Helena calls him hard-hearted and irresistible at once, and says that what he pulls is not iron but a heart true as steel.

Demetrius said, “Do I entice you? Do I speak kindly to you? Or rather, don’t I tell you in the plainest truth that I do not and cannot love you?”

Helena said, “And for that very reason I love you all the more. I am your spaniel; and, Demetrius, the more you beat me, the more I will fawn on you. Just treat me as your spaniel: kick me, strike me, neglect me, lose me; only give me permission, unworthy as I am, to follow you. What worse place could I beg for in your love, and yet to me a place of high respect, than to be treated as you treat your dog?”

Demetrius said, “Don’t push the hatred in my spirit too far; for I am sick when I look at you.”

Helena said, “And I am sick when I do not look at you.”

Demetrius said, “You put your modesty too much in question, leaving the city and putting yourself into the hands of a man who does not love you; trusting the opportunity of night and the evil suggestions of a deserted place with the rich treasure of your virginity.”

Helena said, “Your virtue is my safeguard, my licence to be here. Because it is not night when I see your face, I do not think I am in the night; nor does this wood lack worlds of company, for to me you are the whole world. Then how can anyone say I am alone, when the whole world is here to look at me?”

Demetrius said, “I’ll run away from you and hide in the thickets, and leave you to the mercy of wild beasts.”

Helena said, “The wildest of them has no heart as cruel as yours. Run when you like: the story will be turned around. Apollo runs away, and Daphne gives chase; the dove pursues the griffin; the gentle hind hurries to catch the tiger; useless speed, when cowardice pursues and courage runs away.”

Michael, the guide: In the myth told by the Roman poet Ovid, the god Apollo chased the nymph Daphne, who escaped him only by being changed into a laurel tree; Helena turns the story round so that the woman chases. She reverses the natural order twice more: a griffin was a legendary beast, part eagle and part lion, and a hind is a female deer.

Demetrius said, “I won’t stay here to listen to your talk; let me go. Or, if you follow me, don’t doubt that I will do you harm in the wood.”

Helena said, “Yes, in the temple, in the town, in the field, you do me harm. For shame, Demetrius! The wrongs you do me bring scandal on my whole sex. We cannot fight for love, as men may do; we should be wooed, and were not made to woo.” Demetrius left, and Helena said, “I’ll follow you, and make a heaven out of hell, to die by the hand I love so well.” And she left.

Oberon said, “Farewell, nymph. Before he leaves this grove, you shall run from him, and he shall seek your love.”

Puck came back.

Oberon said, “Have you got the flower there? Welcome, wanderer.”

Puck said, “Yes, here it is.”

Oberon said, “Please, give it to me. I know a bank where the wild thyme blooms, where oxlips and the nodding violet grow, all roofed over with lush woodbine, the honeysuckle, with sweet musk roses and with eglantine, the sweetbriar. There Titania sleeps for part of the night, lulled among these flowers with dances and delight; and there the snake sheds her glossy, enamelled skin, a garment wide enough to wrap a fairy in. And with the juice of this I’ll streak her eyes, and fill her full of hateful fantasies, fancies for loathsome things. You take some of it, and search through this grove. A sweet Athenian lady is in love with a scornful young man: anoint his eyes, but do it when the next thing he sees will be the lady. You will know the man by the Athenian clothes he has on. Do it with some care, so that he turns out more doting on her than she is on her love. And make sure you meet me before the first cock crows.”

Puck said, “Don’t worry, my lord; your servant will do it.” And they went off.
