# Chapter 5 — Bottom and the fairy queen

In the wood, where Titania still lay asleep, Quince, Snug, Bottom, Flute, Snout, and Starveling came in.

Bottom said, “Are we all here?”

Quince said, “Right on time, right on time. And here’s a marvellously convenient place for our rehearsal. This green patch of grass shall be our stage, and this hawthorn thicket our tiring-house, where we get dressed; and we will act it out just as we will do it in front of the duke.”

Bottom said, “Peter Quince—”

Quince said, “What do you say, bully Bottom, my fine fellow?”

Bottom said, “There are things in this comedy of Pyramus and Thisby that will never please. First, Pyramus must draw a sword to kill himself, which the ladies cannot bear. What’s your answer to that?”

Snout said, “By our Lady, that’s a perilous fear.”

Starveling said, “I believe we must leave the killing out, when all’s said and done.”

Bottom said, “Not a bit of it. I have a scheme to make everything all right. Write me a prologue, and let the prologue seem to say that we will do no harm with our swords, and that Pyramus is not really killed; and, for the more better assurance, tell them that I, Pyramus, am not Pyramus, but Bottom the weaver. This will put them out of fear.”

Quince said, “Well, we will have such a prologue, and it shall be written in eight and six.”

Bottom said, “No, make it two more; let it be written in eight and eight.”

Michael, the guide: Eight and six is the common ballad measure, with lines of eight syllables and six syllables taking turns. Bottom wants two more syllables, so that every line has eight.

Snout said, “Won’t the ladies be afraid of the lion?”

Starveling said, “I fear it, I promise you.”

Bottom said, “Masters, you ought to think this over among yourselves. To bring in, God shield us, a lion among ladies is a most dreadful thing; for there is no more fearful wild-fowl living than your lion; and we ought to see to it.”

Snout said, “Then another prologue must tell them he is not a lion.”

Bottom said, “No, you must name his name, and half his face must be seen through the lion’s neck; and he himself must speak through it, saying something like this, or to the same defect: ‘Ladies,’ or ‘Fair ladies, I would wish you,’ or ‘I would request you,’ or ‘I would entreat you, not to fear, not to tremble: my life for yours, I stake my own life on your safety. If you think I come here as a lion, it would be a pity of my life; my own life would be in a sorry way. No, I am no such thing; I am a man as other men are.’ And there, indeed, let him name his name, and tell them plainly that he is Snug the joiner.”

Michael, the guide: Bottom’s slips are part of the fun: he calls the lion a “wild-fowl,” a word for game birds, and says “to the same defect” when he means “effect.” A joiner is a woodworker, so Snug is to show half his face and introduce himself by his everyday trade.

Quince said, “Well, it shall be so. But there is two hard things. That is, to bring the moonlight into a room; for, you know, Pyramus and Thisby meet by moonlight.”

Snout said, “Does the moon shine on the night we perform our play?”

Bottom said, “A calendar, a calendar! Look in the almanac; find out moonshine, find out moonshine!”

Quince said, “Yes, it does shine that night.”

Bottom said, “Why, then you may leave a casement, one of the opening panes of the great chamber window where we play, open, and the moon may shine in at the casement.”

Quince said, “Yes; or else someone must come in with a bundle of thorns and a lantern, and say he comes to disfigure, or to present, the person of Moonshine. Then there is another thing: we must have a wall in the great chamber; for Pyramus and Thisby, says the story, talked through the chink of a wall.”

Snout said, “You can never bring in a wall. What do you say, Bottom?”

Bottom said, “Some man or other must play Wall; and let him have some plaster, or some loam, or some rough-cast, that gritty plaster, about him, to signify wall; and let him hold his fingers like this, and through that cranny Pyramus and Thisby shall whisper.”

Michael, the guide: Quince says “disfigure” when he means “figure,” in the sense of represent. The man in the moon was traditionally pictured carrying a bundle of thorns, which is why Moonshine needs one. The players’ almanac says the moon will shine on their performance night, although at the start of the story the wedding was said to come with a new moon; the play lets both stand.

Quince said, “If that can be done, then all is well. Come, sit down, every mother’s son, and rehearse your parts. Pyramus, you begin; when you have spoken your speech, go into that thicket; and so on, every one according to his cue.”

Puck came in behind them.

Puck said, “What rough bumpkins in homespun hemp are these, swaggering about so close to the fairy queen’s cradle, where she lies? What, a play about to begin! I’ll be one of the audience; an actor too, perhaps, if I see a reason.”

Quince said, “Speak, Pyramus. Thisby, stand forward.”

Bottom, speaking as Pyramus, said, “Thisby, the flowers of odious savours sweet—”

Quince said, “Odours, odours.”

Bottom began again: “—odours savours sweet: so has your breath, my dearest Thisby dear. But hark, a voice! Just stay here a while, and soon I will appear to you.” And he went off into the thicket.

Puck said, “A stranger Pyramus than ever played here!” And he went off too.

Flute said, “Must I speak now?”

Quince said, “Yes, indeed you must; for you must understand that he only goes to see a noise that he heard, and is to come back again.”

Flute, speaking as Thisby, said, “Most radiant Pyramus, most lily-white of hue, of colour like the red rose on the triumphant briar, most brisky juvenal, and also most lovely Jew, as true as truest horse, that yet would never tire, I’ll meet you, Pyramus, at Ninny’s tomb.”

Quince said, “‘Ninus’ tomb,’ man! Why, you mustn’t say that yet; that is what you answer to Pyramus. You are speaking your whole part at once, cues and all. Pyramus, enter: your cue is past; it is ‘never tire.’”

Flute, speaking as Thisby again, said, “Oh— as true as truest horse, that yet would never tire.”

Michael, the guide: Bottom says “odious,” meaning hateful, where the script means “odorous,” sweet-smelling, and even after Quince’s correction he keeps the muddled “savours.” Flute’s Thisby calls Pyramus a “brisky juvenal,” a lively youth, and a “Jew,” probably just for the jingle, and says “Ninny,” a word for a fool, instead of Ninus, a legendary ancient king at whose tomb the lovers meet in the old tale. He also recites straight through Pyramus’s cue, “never tire,” instead of stopping for Pyramus to come back.

Puck came back, and with him came Bottom, now wearing an ass’s head.

Bottom, speaking Pyramus’s line, said, “If I were fair, Thisby, I would be only yours.”

Quince cried, “Oh, monstrous! Oh, strange! We are haunted. Pray, masters! Run, masters! Help!” And Quince, Snug, Flute, Snout, and Starveling all fled.

Puck said, “I’ll follow you; I’ll lead you round and round, through bog, through bush, through thicket, through briar. Sometimes I’ll be a horse, sometimes a hound, a hog, a headless bear, sometimes a fire; and I’ll neigh, and bark, and grunt, and roar, and burn, like horse, hound, hog, bear, fire, at every turn.” And he left.

Bottom said, “Why do they run away? This is a knavery of theirs, a trick to make me afraid.”

Snout came back and said, “O Bottom, you are changed! What do I see on you?”

Bottom said, “What do you see? You see an ass-head of your own, do you?” And Snout left.

Quince came back and said, “Bless you, Bottom! Bless you! You are translated.” And he left.

Michael, the guide: The play never shows how Bottom got the ass’s head; we only know that Puck, who said he might become an actor in this play, went off after Bottom and came back with him. “Translated” here means transformed. When Bottom tells Snout he sees “an ass-head of your own,” he means the ordinary insult that Snout is the fool, not knowing the words are true of himself.

Bottom said, “I see their knavery: this is to make an ass of me, to frighten me, if they could. But I will not stir from this place, whatever they do. I will walk up and down here, and I will sing, so that they shall hear I am not afraid.” And he sang:
“The ousel cock, so black of hue,
With orange-tawny bill,
The throstle with his note so true,
The wren with little quill;”

Titania woke and said, “What angel wakes me from my flowery bed?”

Bottom sang on:
“The finch, the sparrow, and the lark,
The plain-song cuckoo grey,
Whose note full many a man does mark,
And dares not answer nay;”
And he added, “For indeed, who would set his wits against so foolish a bird? Who would call a bird a liar, however often it cries ‘cuckoo’?”

Michael, the guide: In Bottom’s song the ousel is the blackbird and the throstle the thrush; the wren’s “little quill” probably means its small, piping note. The cuckoo’s plain-song is its simple, unchanging call, and “cuckoo” was heard as “cuckold,” the old mocking word for a man whose wife is unfaithful, so many a man hears it and dares not say no. Bottom’s joke is that nobody would bother arguing with so foolish a bird.

Titania said, “I beg you, gentle mortal, sing again. My ear is deeply in love with your voice; and so my eye is held spellbound by your shape; and the power of your fine qualities forces me, at first sight, to say, to swear, I love you.”

Michael, the guide: This is Oberon’s charm at work. Titania woke to Bottom’s singing, so the ass-headed weaver is the first creature she has seen, and she calls him an angel; Oberon had wished she would wake when some vile thing was near.

Bottom said, “I think, mistress, you have little reason for that. And yet, to tell the truth, reason and love keep little company together these days; more’s the pity that some honest neighbours won’t make them friends. There, you see, I can gleek, crack a joke, when the occasion comes.”

Titania said, “You are as wise as you are beautiful.”

Bottom said, “Not so, either; but if I had wit enough to get out of this wood, I’d have enough to serve my own turn, enough for my own needs.”

Titania said, “Do not wish to leave this wood. You shall stay here, whether you want to or not. I am a spirit of no common rank; summer itself always waits upon my royal state; and I love you. So come with me. I’ll give you fairies to wait on you, and they shall fetch you jewels from the deep, and sing while you sleep on pressed flowers. And I will purge away your mortal grossness, the heavy, earthly stuff of your mortal body, so that you shall move like an airy spirit. Peaseblossom! Cobweb! Moth! And Mustardseed!”

In came Peaseblossom, Cobweb, Moth, and Mustardseed.

The first fairy said, “Ready.”

A second said, “And I.”

A third said, “And I.”

And a fourth said, “And I.”

Then all four fairies asked together, “Where shall we go?”

Titania said, “Be kind and courteous to this gentleman. Hop where he walks and frolic before his eyes; feed him apricots and dewberries, purple grapes, green figs, and mulberries; steal the honey-bags from the bumble-bees, and for night candles snip off their waxy thighs and light them at the fiery glow-worm’s eyes, to light my love to bed and to his rising; and pluck the wings from painted butterflies to fan the moonbeams from his sleeping eyes. Bow to him, elves, and pay him your courtesies.”

The first fairy said, “Hail, mortal!”

The second fairy said, “Hail!”

The third fairy said, “Hail!”

And the fourth fairy said, “Hail!”

Bottom said, “I humbly beg your worships’ pardon, with all my heart. May I ask your worship’s name?”

One fairy answered, “Cobweb.”

Bottom said, “I shall want to know you better, good Master Cobweb; if I cut my finger, I shall make bold with you. Your name, honest gentleman?”

Another answered, “Peaseblossom.”

Bottom said, “Please give my regards to Mistress Squash, your mother, and to Master Peascod, your father. Good Master Peaseblossom, I shall want to know you better too. Your name, I beg you, sir?”

Another answered, “Mustardseed.”

Bottom said, “Good Master Mustardseed, I know your patience well, all you have put up with: that same cowardly, giant-like ox-beef has devoured many a gentleman of your family. I promise you, your relatives have made my eyes water before now. I hope to know you better, good Master Mustardseed.”

Michael, the guide: Cobwebs were used to stop the bleeding of small cuts, hence Bottom making bold with Cobweb if he cuts his finger. A squash is an unripe pea pod and a peascod a full one, so he makes them Peaseblossom’s parents; and mustard was eaten with beef, so he pities Mustardseed’s relatives, devoured alongside it, who have made his eyes water.

Titania said, “Come, wait on him; lead him to my bower. The moon, I think, looks down with a watery eye; and when she weeps, every little flower weeps too, lamenting some enforced chastity. Tie up my love’s tongue, and bring him in silence.” And they all left.

Michael, the guide: The moon’s watery eye probably means the dew, pictured as her tears, and in the poetry of the time the moon was linked with chastity. “Enforced chastity” can be read two ways, as chastity violated by force or as chastity forced on someone against their will, and Titania doesn’t say which. Her last order is that Bottom be silenced and led away.
