# Chapter 7 — Four lovers at cross-purposes

Lysander and Helena came in.

Lysander said, “Why should you think that I would court you in scorn? Scorn and mockery never come in tears. Look: when I make a vow, I weep; and vows born that way show all their truth at the very moment of their birth. How can these things in me seem like scorn to you, when they carry the badge of faith to prove them true?”

Helena said, “You push your cunning further and further. When one truth kills another truth, oh, what a fight, devilish and holy at once! These vows belong to Hermia: will you abandon her? Weigh one oath against the other and you will be weighing nothing. Put your vows to her and your vows to me in two scales, and they will balance exactly, and both be as light as idle stories.”

Michael, the guide: The “badge of faith” Lysander points to is his tears, which he says prove his vows sincere. Helena answers that his new vows to her must kill his old vows to Hermia, a fight that is holy because it is fought between vows and devilish because one must betray the other; and since the two sets of oaths cancel each other out, a balance would show they weigh nothing.

Lysander said, “I had no judgement when I swore to her.”

Helena said, “Nor have you any now, in my opinion, when you abandon her.”

Lysander said, “Demetrius loves her, and he does not love you.”

Demetrius woke, and said, “Oh, Helen, goddess, nymph, perfect, divine! To what, my love, shall I compare your eyes? Crystal is muddy beside them. Oh, how ripe your lips look, those kissing cherries, how tempting they grow! That pure frozen white, the snow on the high Taurus mountains, fanned by the east wind, turns black as a crow when you hold up your hand. Oh, let me kiss this princess of pure white, this seal of bliss!”

Michael, the guide: Demetrius wakes speaking to Helena, as Oberon’s charm on his eyes intended. The Taurus are a high mountain range in what is now southern Turkey, and their snow is his picture of perfect whiteness, which looks black as a crow beside Helena’s hand; he calls that hand a “seal of bliss,” like a seal that confirms a promise of happiness.

Helena said, “Oh, spite! Oh, hell! I see you are all set on ganging up against me for your amusement. If you were civilised and knew courtesy, you would not do me so much injury. Can’t you simply hate me, as I know you do, without joining together heart and soul to mock me too? If you were men, as you are men in appearance, you would not treat a gentle lady like this: vowing and swearing and overpraising my qualities, when I am sure you hate me in your hearts. You are both rivals, and you love Hermia; and now you are both rivals in mocking Helena. A fine exploit, a manly enterprise, to conjure up tears in a poor girl’s eyes with your derision! No one of noble birth would offend a maiden like this, and wring a poor soul’s patience out of her, all to make yourselves some sport.”

Lysander said, “You are being unkind, Demetrius; don’t be. For you love Hermia; you know that I know it. And here, with all good will, with all my heart, I give up to you my share in Hermia’s love; and you hand over to me your share in Helena, whom I love, and will love until I die.”

Helena said, “Never did mockers waste more empty breath.”

Demetrius said, “Lysander, keep your Hermia; I want none of her. If I ever loved her, all that love is gone. My heart only stayed with her like a guest on a visit, and now it has come back home to Helen, there to remain.”

Lysander said, “Helen, it is not so.”

Demetrius said, “Don’t belittle a faith you know nothing about, or you will pay dearly for it, at your peril. Look, here comes your love; there is your darling.”

Hermia came back.

Hermia said, “Dark night, which takes away the eye’s power to see, makes the ear quicker to understand; where it weakens the sense of sight, it repays the hearing twice over. It was not my eye that found you, Lysander; my ear, and I thank it, brought me to the sound of you. But why did you leave me so unkindly?”

Lysander said, “Why should a man stay, when love urges him to go?”

Hermia said, “What love could urge Lysander away from my side?”

Lysander said, “Lysander’s love, which would not let him stay: fair Helena, who gilds the night more brightly than all those fiery circles and eyes of light up there. Why do you come looking for me? Couldn’t this make you understand that it was the hate I bear you that made me leave you like that?”

Michael, the guide: “Oes” were small round spangles sewn on to clothes to glitter. Lysander uses them, and “eyes of light,” for the stars, and claims Helena lights up the night more richly than they do.

Hermia said, “You don’t mean what you say; it cannot be.”

Helena said, “Look, she is one of this conspiracy! Now I see that all three of them have joined together to stage this false game to spite me. Hurtful Hermia! Most ungrateful girl! Have you plotted, have you schemed with these men to torment me with this foul mockery? All the confidences we two have shared, the vows we made as sisters, the hours we have spent together when we scolded swift-footed time for parting us — oh, is all of it forgotten? All our school-days’ friendship, our childhood innocence?

“We, Hermia, like two creative gods, have made one flower together with our needles, both working on one sampler, sitting on one cushion, both singing one song, both in one key, as if our hands, our sides, our voices and our minds had all been one body. So we grew together, like a double cherry, seeming parted, and yet united in our separation: two lovely berries moulded on one stem; so, with two bodies to look at, but one heart; like two coats of arms in heraldry that belong to only one owner and are crowned with one crest. And will you tear our old love apart, to join with men in scorning your poor friend? It is not friendly, it is not maidenly. All women, as well as I, may scold you for it, though I alone feel the injury.”

Michael, the guide: A sampler was a piece of practice embroidery, and Helena calls the two girls “artificial gods,” meaning creators skilled in an art, as they stitched one flower together. Her heraldry image is of a shield displaying two coats of arms that still belong to a single owner beneath a single crest: two bodies, one heart. The exact heraldic sense of her phrase “two of the first” is not certain.

Hermia said, “I am astonished by your passionate words. I don’t scorn you; it seems that you scorn me.”

Helena said, “Haven’t you set Lysander on, as if to scorn me, to follow me and praise my eyes and face? And made your other love, Demetrius, who only just now was spurning me with his foot, call me goddess, nymph, divine and rare, precious, heavenly? Why does he say this to the woman he hates? And why does Lysander deny his love for you, which is so rich in his soul, and offer me affection, if you please, unless you set him on and gave your consent? So what if I am not as favoured as you, not so hung about with love, so fortunate, but most miserable, loving and not loved? You should pity this rather than despise it.”

Hermia said, “I don’t understand what you mean by this.”

Helena said, “Yes, go on, keep it up, put on your fake serious faces, pull faces at me when I turn my back, wink at each other, keep the sweet joke going. A game carried off this well deserves to be written into the history books. If you had any pity, grace, or manners, you would not make me the butt of such a joke. But farewell. It is partly my own fault, and death or absence will soon put that right.”

Lysander said, “Stay, gentle Helena; hear my excuse: my love, my life, my soul, fair Helena!”

Helena said, “Oh, excellent!”

Hermia said, “My sweet, don’t mock her like that.”

Demetrius said, “If her pleading can’t make you stop, I can force you.”

Lysander said, “You can no more force me than she can move me by pleading. Your threats have no more strength than her feeble prayers. Helen, I love you; by my life, I do. I swear by that life, which I am ready to lose for you, to prove false any man who says I do not love you.”

Demetrius said, “I say I love you more than he can.”

Lysander said, “If you say so, step aside with me, and prove it too.”

Demetrius said, “Quick, come on!”

Hermia said, “Lysander, what is all this leading to?”

Lysander said, “Away, you Ethiope!”

Demetrius said, “No, no; he’ll —” and then, to Lysander, “Pretend to break loose; carry on as if you would follow me, but don’t actually come. You are a tame man. Go!”

Lysander said, “Let go, you cat, you burr! Vile thing, let go, or I will shake you off me like a serpent!”

Hermia said, “Why have you grown so rough? What change is this? Sweet love —”

Lysander said, “Your love? Out, tawny Tartar, out! Out, loathsome medicine! Hated potion, away!”

Michael, the guide: Lysander now insults Hermia with comparisons to dark colouring, set against what he calls “fair” Helena, a word that can mean both light-skinned and beautiful. An “Ethiope” is an Ethiopian, an African, and a “tawny Tartar” is a brown-skinned Tartar. These are ugly, prejudiced insults, meant to wound, and they are Lysander’s words rather than a description the play gives elsewhere in this scene. His “cat” and “burr,” a burr being a prickly seed head that clings to clothing, suggest Hermia is holding on to him, as Demetrius’s taunt about pretending to break loose also implies; and he calls her a hated potion, though he is the one under a love-juice.

Hermia said, “Aren’t you joking?”

Helena said, “Yes, indeed; and so are you.”

Lysander said, “Demetrius, I will keep my word to you.”

Demetrius said, “I wish I had your bond, for I can see a weak bond holds you. I won’t trust your word.”

Michael, the guide: Demetrius is punning on “bond.” He would rather have Lysander’s bond, a signed legal pledge, than his spoken word, because the only bond he can see is the weak one holding Lysander back, presumably Hermia’s hold on him.

Lysander said, “What, should I hurt her, strike her, kill her dead? Although I hate her, I won’t harm her like that.”

Hermia said, “What, can you do me greater harm than hate? Hate me! Why? Oh, what news is this, my love? Am I not Hermia? Are you not Lysander? I am as beautiful now as I was a little while ago. This very night you loved me; yet this very night you left me. Why, then you left me — oh, the gods forbid! — in earnest, must I say?”

Lysander said, “Yes, by my life; and I never wanted to see you again. So give up hope, give up questions, give up doubt. Be certain: nothing is truer; it is no joke that I hate you and love Helena.”

Hermia said, “Oh, no! You trickster! You canker-blossom! You thief of love! What, have you come by night and stolen my love’s heart from him?”

Michael, the guide: A juggler, Hermia’s word here, meant a trickster or conjuror who deceives by sleight of hand. A canker-blossom is a canker worm, a grub that eats a flower from inside the bud, and Hermia accuses Helena of destroying her love that way.

Helena said, “Very fine, upon my word! Have you no modesty, no maidenly shame, no trace of bashfulness? What, will you tear impatient answers out of my gentle tongue? Shame, shame! You fake, you puppet, you!”

Hermia said, “Puppet? Why that? Oh, so that’s the game. Now I see she has made comparisons between our heights; she has pressed the point of her height; and with her figure, her tall figure, her height, if you please, she has won him over. And have you grown so high in his esteem because I am so dwarfish and so low? How low am I, you painted maypole? Speak! How low am I? I am not yet so low that my nails can’t reach your eyes.”

Michael, the guide: Coming straight after “counterfeit,” Helena’s “puppet” may mean a fake, or a doll acting a part, though her exact sense is not certain; Hermia, at any rate, takes it as a jab at her small size. From here the quarrel turns on height: Hermia is the shorter, and she calls tall Helena a painted maypole, a long thin painted pole, probably also hinting that Helena paints her face.

Helena said, “I beg you, gentlemen, though you mock me, don’t let her hurt me. I was never a scold; I have no talent at all for shrewishness; in my cowardice I am a true girl. Don’t let her strike me. Perhaps you think, because she is somewhat lower than I am, that I can match her.”

Hermia said, “Lower! Listen, again!”

Helena said, “Good Hermia, don’t be so bitter with me. I have always loved you, Hermia, always kept your secrets, never wronged you; except that, out of love for Demetrius, I told him you were stealing away to this wood. He followed you; for love, I followed him; but he has scolded me away, and threatened to strike me, kick me, yes, even to kill me. And now, if you will let me go in peace, I will carry my foolishness back to Athens and follow you no further. Let me go. You see how simple and how foolish I am.”

Hermia said, “Why, get going. Who is stopping you?”

Helena said, “A foolish heart, which I am leaving behind here.”

Hermia said, “What, with Lysander?”

Helena said, “With Demetrius.”

Lysander said, “Don’t be afraid; she shall not harm you, Helena.”

Demetrius said, “No, sir, she shall not, even if you should take Hermia’s side.”

Helena said, “Oh, when she’s angry, she is sharp and vicious! She was a vixen when she went to school; and though she is only little, she is fierce.”

Hermia said, “Little again! Nothing but low and little! Why will you let her insult me like this? Let me get at her.”

Lysander said, “Get away, you dwarf; you minimus, made of stunting knot-grass; you bead, you acorn.”

Michael, the guide: “Minimus” is Latin for the smallest, so a tiny creature. Knot-grass is a low, creeping weed that was believed to stunt growth, so Lysander calls Hermia a stunted thing made of it.

Demetrius said, “You are too officious on behalf of a woman who scorns your services. Leave her alone; don’t speak of Helena; don’t take her side. For if you intend to show her ever so little love, you shall pay for it.”

Lysander said, “Now she isn’t holding me. Now follow, if you dare, to put it to the test whose claim to Helena is stronger, yours or mine.”

Demetrius said, “Follow? No, I’ll go with you, side by side, cheek by jowl.” And Lysander and Demetrius left.

Hermia said, “You, mistress, all this turmoil is because of you. No, don’t back away.”

Helena said, “I won’t trust you, not I, nor stay any longer in your bad-tempered company. Your hands are quicker than mine in a fight; but my legs are longer, to run away.” And she left.

Hermia said, “I am astonished, and don’t know what to say.” And she left.
