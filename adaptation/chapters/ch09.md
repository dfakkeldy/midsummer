# Chapter 9 — The enchantments undone

Lysander, Demetrius, Helena and Hermia were still lying asleep in the same place. Titania came in with Bottom, attended by Peaseblossom, Cobweb, Moth, Mustardseed and other fairies. Oberon was there too, behind them, unseen.

Titania said, “Come, sit down on this flowery bed, while I caress your lovely cheeks, and stick musk-roses in your sleek, smooth head, and kiss your fair, large ears, my gentle joy.”

Bottom said, “Where’s Peaseblossom?”

Peaseblossom said, “Ready.”

Bottom said, “Scratch my head, Peaseblossom. Where’s Mounsieur Cobweb?”

Cobweb said, “Ready.”

Bottom said, “Mounsieur Cobweb, good mounsieur, take your weapons in your hand and kill me a red-hipped bumblebee on the top of a thistle; and, good mounsieur, bring me its honey-bag. Don’t wear yourself out too much in the action, mounsieur; and, good mounsieur, take care the honey-bag doesn’t break. I would hate to have you drowned in a flood of honey, signior. Where’s Mounsieur Mustardseed?”

Mustardseed said, “Ready.”

Bottom said, “Give me your fist, Mounsieur Mustardseed. Please, never mind the courtesies, good mounsieur.”

Mustardseed said, “What do you wish?”

Bottom said, “Nothing, good mounsieur, but to help Cavalery Cobweb scratch. I must go to the barber’s, mounsieur, for I think I am marvellously hairy about the face; and I am such a tender ass, if my hair so much as tickles me, I have to scratch.”

Michael, the guide: “Mounsieur” is an old English spelling of the French “monsieur,” and Bottom uses the title grandly, over and over, mixing in the Italian “signior” and “Cavalery,” apparently his version of “cavalier,” a gentleman-knight; a “neaf” is a fist. He doesn’t seem to realize how literally true it is when he calls himself hairy about the face and a tender ass. Notice, too, that he asks for help for Cobweb to scratch, though he has just sent Cobweb after the bee and given the scratching to Peaseblossom; the text says Cobweb, slip and all.

Titania said, “What, would you like to hear some music, my sweet love?”

Bottom said, “I have a reasonably good ear for music. Let’s have the tongs and the bones.”

Titania said, “Or tell me, sweet love, what you would like to eat.”

Bottom said, “Truly, a peck of fodder. I could munch your good dry oats. I think I have a great longing for a bottle of hay. Good hay, sweet hay, has no equal.”

Titania said, “I have an adventurous fairy who shall seek out the squirrel’s hoard and fetch you new nuts.”

Bottom said, “I’d rather have a handful or two of dried peas. But please, let none of your people disturb me: I have an exposition of sleep come upon me.”

Michael, the guide: The tongs and the bones were homely country music: a pair of tongs struck to ring out a rhythm, and bones clacked together in the hand. A peck is a measure of grain, a “bottle” of hay is a bundle of it, and every food Bottom asks for is a donkey’s fodder. “Exposition of sleep” is another of his muddled words; he seems to mean a disposition, an urge, to sleep.

Titania said, “Sleep, and I will wind you in my arms. Fairies, be gone, and away in every direction.” The fairies left. “Just so the woodbine gently twines around the sweet honeysuckle; so the female ivy rings the bark-covered fingers of the elm. Oh, how I love you! How I dote on you!” And they slept.

Michael, the guide: “Woodbine” and “honeysuckle” could name the same plant, so Titania’s first picture may be of one climbing plant twined around another; exactly which plants she means is uncertain. She calls the ivy female and pictures it ringing the elm’s twigs like fingers: both images are of clinging plants wrapped around their support, as she winds Bottom in her arms.

Puck came in.

Oberon came forward and said, “Welcome, good Robin. Do you see this sweet sight? I am beginning now to pity her infatuation. For when I met her lately, out behind the wood, gathering sweet love-tokens for this hateful fool, I scolded her and quarrelled with her; for she had crowned his hairy temples with a coronet of fresh and fragrant flowers. And the same dew that used sometimes to swell on the buds like round, lustrous pearls of the East now stood in the pretty little flowers’ eyes like tears lamenting their own disgrace. When I had taunted her as much as I pleased, and she had mildly begged my patience, I asked her for her changeling child. She gave him to me at once, and sent her fairy to carry him to my bower in fairyland.

“And now that I have the boy, I will undo this hateful defect in her eyes. And, gentle Puck, take this transformed scalp off the head of this Athenian rustic, so that, waking when the others do, they may all make their way back to Athens, and think of this night’s happenings as nothing more than the fierce torment of a dream. But first I will release the fairy queen.”

Then Oberon spoke the charm: “Be as you used to be; see as you used to see. Diana’s bud over Cupid’s flower has such force and blessed power.” And he said, “Now, my Titania, wake, my sweet queen.”

Michael, the guide: Diana is the Roman goddess of the moon and the hunt, and of chastity; Cupid is the god of love. Oberon’s remedy is “Diana’s bud,” and its power wins out over “Cupid’s flower,” the love-juice that made Titania dote. Everything he reports about meeting Titania and getting the boy happened out of our sight; we have only his account.

Titania said, “My Oberon! What visions I have seen! I thought I was in love with an ass.”

Oberon said, “There lies your love.”

Titania said, “How did these things come about? Oh, how my eyes loathe his face now!”

Oberon said, “Silence for a while. Robin, take off this head. Titania, call for music, and strike the senses of all these five deader than ordinary sleep.”

Titania said, “Music, ho! Music, such as charms sleep!” And soft, quiet music played.

Puck said, “Now, when you wake, peep out with your own fool’s eyes.”

Oberon said, “Sound, music! Come, my queen, take hands with me, and rock the ground where these sleepers lie. Now you and I are friends again, and tomorrow at midnight we will dance solemnly and triumphantly in Duke Theseus’ house, and bless it with every fair prosperity. There the pairs of faithful lovers shall be married, along with Theseus, all in joy.”

Michael, the guide: “These five” are the four sleeping lovers and Bottom. Oberon speaks of dancing tomorrow at midnight; that doesn’t line up neatly with the four days Theseus spoke of at the very start, and the play leaves its timing as it is. His “pairs of faithful lovers” is his own prediction: Demetrius has still not been given the remedy.

Puck said, “Fairy king, take heed and listen: I hear the morning lark.”

Oberon said, “Then, my queen, in solemn silence, let us trip away after the shadow of the night. We can circle the globe sooner than the wandering moon.”

Titania said, “Come, my lord, and as we fly, tell me how it happened tonight that I was found sleeping here with these mortals on the ground.”

Horns sounded from somewhere out of sight, and Oberon, Titania and Puck left.
