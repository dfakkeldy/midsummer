# Chapter 6 — The mistake comes to light

In another part of the wood, Oberon came in.

Oberon said, “I wonder whether Titania has woken yet; and then, what it was that first came before her eyes, which she must now dote on to the utmost.”

Puck came in.

Oberon said, “Here comes my messenger. Well now, mad spirit! What night-time mischief is going on now in this haunted grove?”

Puck said, “My mistress is in love with a monster. Near her secluded, sacred bower, while she was in her drowsy hour of sleep, a crew of clowns, rough working men who earn their bread at market stalls in Athens, had met together to rehearse a play intended for great Theseus’s wedding day. The shallowest, thickest-skulled dolt of that dull-witted lot, the one who played Pyramus in their entertainment, left his scene and went into a thicket; and when I caught him at that advantage, I fixed an ass’s head on him. Soon his Thisby had to be answered, and out comes my actor.

“When they spy him, then, just as wild geese that see the fowler creeping up on them, or a great flock of russet-headed choughs, rising and cawing at the crack of a gun, scatter and sweep madly across the sky, so his fellows fly away at the sight of him; and at our stamping, here one falls over and over, cries murder, and calls for help from Athens. Their wits so weak, and lost in fears so strong, made senseless things begin to do them harm; for briars and thorns snatch at their clothes, some at sleeves, some at hats; everything grabs at those who give way. I led them on in this distracted fear and left sweet Pyramus there, transformed; and at that very moment, as it happened, Titania woke and straightaway fell in love with an ass.”

Michael, the guide: Puck calls the players “patches,” meaning clowns, and “rude mechanicals,” rough working men. Choughs are birds of the crow family, here with russet-coloured heads. This is Puck’s own account of how Bottom got the ass’s head; the tumbling and the cries of murder are things he reports, which we did not see ourselves.

Oberon said, “This turns out better than I could have planned. But have you yet laid the love-juice on the Athenian’s eyes, as I told you to?”

Puck said, “I caught him sleeping; that is finished too. And the Athenian woman was lying by his side, so that when he woke, she would have to be the one he saw.”

Hermia and Demetrius came in.

Oberon said, “Keep hidden. This is the same Athenian.”

Puck said, “This is the woman, but this is not the man.”

Demetrius said, “Oh, why do you rebuke someone who loves you so much? Save breath as bitter as that for your bitter enemy.”

Hermia said, “For now I only scold you; but I ought to treat you worse, for you, I fear, have given me cause to curse you. If you have killed Lysander in his sleep, then, since you are already in blood over your shoes, plunge in deeper and kill me too. The sun was not as faithful to the day as he was to me. Would he have stolen away from Hermia while she slept? I would as soon believe that this whole earth could be bored right through, and that the moon could creep through the centre and so spoil her brother’s noonday among the Antipodes. It can only be that you have murdered him; that is how a murderer should look, so deathly, so grim.”

Michael, the guide: In old myth the moon goddess was the sun god’s sister, so the moon’s “brother” is the sun. The Antipodes are the people on the opposite side of the world; Hermia pictures the moon creeping through a hole bored through the earth and intruding on the sun’s noonday over there, and says she would believe that as soon as believe Lysander left her sleeping.

Demetrius said, “That is how the murdered should look, and so should I, pierced through the heart by your harsh cruelty. Yet you, the murderer, look as bright and clear as Venus up there in her glimmering sphere.”

Hermia said, “What has this to do with my Lysander? Where is he? Ah, good Demetrius, will you give him back to me?”

Demetrius said, “I would rather give his carcass to my hounds.”

Hermia said, “Out, dog! Out, cur! You drive me beyond the limits of a maiden’s patience. Have you killed him, then? From now on may you never be counted among men! Oh, for once tell the truth, tell the truth, even if only for my sake! Would you have dared to look him in the face while he was awake, and have you killed him while he slept? Oh, what a brave deed! Couldn’t a worm, an adder, do as much? An adder did do it; for no adder ever stung with a more forked tongue than yours, you serpent.”

Michael, the guide: “Worm” could mean any snake. Hermia’s “brave deed” is bitter sarcasm, and her “more forked tongue” plays on the adder’s split tongue and the double, lying tongue she accuses Demetrius of having.

Demetrius said, “You are spending your passion on a mistaken notion. I am not guilty of Lysander’s blood; nor is he dead, for all I know.”

Hermia said, “Then I beg you, tell me that he is well.”

Demetrius said, “And if I could, what would I get for it?”

Hermia said, “The privilege of never seeing me again. And so I leave your hated company: see me no more, whether he is dead or not.” And she left.

Demetrius said, “There’s no following her while she’s in this fierce mood, so I will stay here for a while. Sorrow’s heaviness grows heavier because of the debt that bankrupt sleep owes to sorrow; and sleep will now pay back some small part of it, if I stay here and accept its offer.” And he lay down and slept.

Michael, the guide: Demetrius means that sorrow weighs more when you have gone without sleep. He pictures sleep as a bankrupt debtor that owes sorrow some rest, and by lying down here he lets it pay back a little of what it owes.

Oberon said, “What have you done? You have made a complete mistake and laid the love-juice on some true lover’s eyes. From your mistake there must follow some true love turned false, and not a false love turned true.”

Puck said, “Then fate is in control: for every one man who keeps faith, a million fail, breaking oath upon oath.”

Oberon said, “Go about the wood swifter than the wind, and see that you find Helena of Athens. She is all love-sick and pale-faced, with sighs of love that cost her fresh blood dearly. Bring her here by some illusion; I’ll charm his eyes ready for when she appears.”

Puck said, “I go, I go; look how I go, swifter than an arrow from a Tartar’s bow.” And he left.

Michael, the guide: An old belief held that every sigh drew blood from the heart, which is why Oberon says Helena’s sighs are costing her dearly. The Tartars, horsemen of the central Asian steppes, were famous archers, so Puck boasts that he will fly faster than their arrows.

Oberon said:
“Flower of this purple dye,
Hit by Cupid’s archery,
Sink into the apple of his eye.
When his love he comes to spy,
Let her shine as gloriously
As the Venus of the sky.
When you wake, if she is by,
Beg of her for remedy.”

Michael, the guide: The purple flower is the one Oberon earlier said was struck by Cupid’s arrow, and the “apple” of the eye is the pupil. The charm is meant for the sleeping Demetrius: if Helena is near when he wakes, she is to shine for him as gloriously as Venus in the sky, the planet named after the goddess of love and traditionally called the morning or evening star. He is then to beg her for a remedy, meaning relief from the love-sickness he will feel; the charm does not say how she will answer.

Puck came back.

Puck said, “Captain of our fairy band, Helena is here at hand, and with her the youth I mistook, pleading for a lover’s reward. Shall we watch their foolish show? Lord, what fools these mortals be!”

Oberon said, “Stand aside. The noise they make will make Demetrius wake.”

Puck said, “Then two at once will woo one; that will be a show beyond compare. And the things that please me best are those that happen preposterously.”

Michael, the guide: “Preposterous” originally meant back-to-front, the last part coming first, so Puck is relishing things turned the wrong way round, with two men now set to woo the same woman.
