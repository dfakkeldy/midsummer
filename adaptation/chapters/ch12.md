# Chapter 12 — The wedding play and the fairies’ farewell

In Athens, at the palace of Theseus, Theseus, Hippolyta and Philostrate came in with lords and attendants.

Hippolyta said, “It’s strange, my Theseus, what these lovers are telling us.”

Theseus said, “More strange than true. I can never believe these old fables, these fairy trifles. Lovers and madmen have such seething brains, such shaping fantasies, that they grasp more than cool reason ever understands. The lunatic, the lover and the poet are made up entirely of imagination. One sees more devils than vast hell could hold: that is the madman. The lover, every bit as frantic, sees Helen’s beauty in an Egyptian face. The poet’s eye, rolling in a fine frenzy, glances from heaven to earth and from earth to heaven; and as imagination gives body to the forms of things unknown, the poet’s pen turns them into shapes, and gives to airy nothing a place to live and a name. Strong imagination plays such tricks that if it merely conceives some joy, it dreams up someone who brought that joy; or at night, imagining something to fear, how easily a bush is taken for a bear!”

Hippolyta said, “But the whole story of the night, told over, and all their minds transformed together like that, testifies to more than fancy’s pictures, and grows into something solid and consistent. But however that may be, it is strange and wonderful.”

Michael, the guide: The lovers have evidently told Theseus and Hippolyta about their night in the wood, though we don’t hear them tell it. Helen is Helen of Troy, the legendary beauty, and Theseus’s line assumes, with the prejudice of its time, that only a lover’s frenzy could see her beauty in an Egyptian face. Hippolyta’s answer is that four people telling the whole story over, their minds changed together, amounts to more than one person’s fancy.

Theseus said, “Here come the lovers, full of joy and mirth.” Lysander, Demetrius, Hermia and Helena came in, and Theseus greeted them: “Joy, gentle friends! Joy and fresh days of love go with your hearts!”

Lysander said, “Even more than to us, may joy wait on you in your royal walks, at your table, and in your bed!”

Theseus said, “Come now, what masques, what dances shall we have to wear away this long age of three hours between our after-supper and bedtime? Where is our usual manager of mirth? What revels are in hand? Is there no play to ease the anguish of a torturing hour? Call Philostrate.”

Philostrate said, “Here, mighty Theseus.”

Theseus said, “Tell me, what pastime have you to shorten this evening? What masque? What music? How shall we cheat the lazy time, if not with some delight?”

Philostrate said, “Here is a list of the entertainments that are ready. Choose which your Highness will see first,” and he gave him a paper.

Theseus read aloud: “‘The battle with the Centaurs, to be sung by an Athenian eunuch to the harp.’ We’ll have none of that; I have already told that to my love, in praise of my kinsman Hercules.” He read on: “‘The riot of the tipsy Bacchanals, tearing the Thracian singer in their rage.’ That is an old show; it was played when I last came home from Thebes a conqueror.” He read: “‘The thrice three Muses mourning for the death of Learning, lately deceased in beggary.’ That is some satire, sharp and critical, not suited to a wedding.” He read: “‘A tedious brief scene of young Pyramus and his love Thisbe; very tragical mirth.’ Merry and tragical! Tedious and brief! That is hot ice and wondrously strange snow. How shall we find the harmony in this discord?”

Philostrate said, “There is a play, my lord, some ten words long, which is as brief as any play I have known; but by ten words, my lord, it is too long, which makes it tedious; for in the whole play there is not one word fitting, not one player suited to his part. And tragical, my noble lord, it is, for in it Pyramus kills himself. When I saw that rehearsed, I must confess, it made my eyes water; but no fit of loud laughter ever shed merrier tears.”

Theseus said, “Who are they that play it?”

Philostrate said, “Hard-handed men who work here in Athens, who never laboured with their minds until now; and now they have worn out their unexercised memories with this same play, for your wedding.”

Theseus said, “And we will hear it.”

Philostrate said, “No, my noble lord; it is not for you. I have heard it through, and it is nothing, nothing in the world; unless you can find sport in their efforts, stretched to the limit and learned by heart with cruel pains, to do you service.”

Theseus said, “I will hear that play; for nothing can ever be amiss when simpleness and duty offer it. Go, bring them in; and take your places, ladies.” Philostrate went out.

Hippolyta said, “I don’t love to see poor wretches overburdened, and duty dying in the effort to serve.”

Theseus said, “Why, gentle sweet, you shall see no such thing.”

Hippolyta said, “He says they can do nothing of this kind.”

Theseus said, “The kinder we, then, to give them thanks for nothing. Our sport shall be to take what they mistake; and where poor duty cannot manage, noble consideration values the effort, not the merit. Wherever I have gone, great scholars have meant to greet me with welcomes prepared beforehand; and I have seen them shiver and turn pale, put full stops in the middle of sentences, choke their practised delivery in their fear, and finally break off speechless, without giving me a welcome at all. Believe me, sweet, out of that silence I still picked a welcome; and in the modesty of fearful duty I read as much as in the rattling tongue of saucy, bold eloquence. So love and tongue-tied simplicity, saying least, speak most, as far as I can judge.”

Michael, the guide: An “abridgement” is a pastime that makes the evening seem shorter, and Philostrate’s “brief” is a short list. In the entertainments Theseus reads out, the Centaurs were legendary creatures, half man and half horse, and Hercules was the great strongman of legend, whom Theseus calls his kinsman; the “Thracian singer” torn apart by the drunken followers of Bacchus, god of wine, is Orpheus; and the “thrice three Muses” are the nine goddesses of the arts. Notice too Hippolyta’s “nothing of this kind,” meaning of this sort, which Theseus turns into “the kinder we.”

Philostrate came back and said, “If it please your Grace, the Prologue is ready.”

Theseus said, “Let him come forward.” Trumpets sounded a flourish.

Quince came in to speak the Prologue.

Quince, as the Prologue, said: “If we offend, it is with our good will. That you should think, we come not to offend, but with good will. To show our simple skill, that is the true beginning of our end. Consider, then, we come but in spite. We do not come as meaning to content you, our true intent is. All for your delight, we are not here. That you should here repent you, the actors are at hand; and, by their show, you shall know all, that you are likely to know.”

Theseus said, “This fellow doesn’t stand upon points.”

Lysander said, “He has ridden his prologue like a rough colt; he doesn’t know the stop. A good moral, my lord: it is not enough to speak; you must speak true.”

Hippolyta said, “Indeed, he has played on his prologue like a child on a recorder: a sound, but not under control.”

Theseus said, “His speech was like a tangled chain: nothing broken, but everything disordered. Who is next?”

Michael, the guide: Quince’s Prologue goes wrong entirely through where he pauses. Spoken with the stops in the right places, it would mean: if we offend, we hope you’ll believe we meant no offence, only good will in showing our simple skill; we don’t come in spite, our true intent is all for your delight, and we aren’t here to make you sorry you came. As he delivers it, he tells the Duke the opposite, which is why Theseus says he doesn’t “stand upon points,” meaning punctuation as well as niceties, and Lysander’s “stop” is both a punctuation mark and the halt a well-trained horse knows.

Pyramus and Thisbe, Wall, Moonshine and Lion came in.

Quince, still as the Prologue, said: “Gentlefolk, perhaps you wonder at this show; but go on wondering, until the truth makes all things plain. This man is Pyramus, if you would know; this beautiful lady is Thisby, certainly. This man, with lime and rough plaster, presents Wall, that vile Wall which kept these lovers apart; and through Wall’s chink, poor souls, they are content to whisper, at which let no man wonder. This man, with lantern, dog and bush of thorn, presents Moonshine; for, if you would know, by moonshine these lovers thought it no shame to meet at Ninus’ tomb, there, there to woo. This grisly beast, which is called Lion by name, scared away, or rather frightened, the trusty Thisby as she came first by night; and as she fled, she let her mantle, her cloak, fall, which vile Lion stained with his bloody mouth. Soon comes Pyramus, a sweet youth and tall, and finds his trusty Thisby’s mantle slain; whereupon, with blade, with bloody blameful blade, he bravely broached his boiling bloody breast; and Thisby, waiting in the mulberry’s shade, drew his dagger and died. For all the rest, let Lion, Moonshine, Wall and the two lovers tell it at length, while they are here.” Quince, Thisbe, Lion and Moonshine went out.

Theseus said, “I wonder whether the lion is going to speak.”

Demetrius said, “No wonder, my lord. One lion may, when many—”

Wall said, “In this same interlude it so happens that I, one Snout by name, present a wall; and such a wall, I would have you think, as had in it a cracked hole or chink, through which the lovers, Pyramus and Thisby, often whispered very secretly. This loam, this rough plaster and this stone show that I am that same wall; the truth is so. And this is the cranny, right and sinister, right and left, through which the frightened lovers are to whisper.”

Theseus said, “Would you want lime and hair to speak any better?”

Demetrius said, “It is the wittiest partition I ever heard hold forth, my lord.”

Theseus said, “Pyramus is drawing near the wall. Silence!”

Pyramus came in.

Pyramus said, “O grim-looking night! O night with hue so black! O night, which always is when day is not! O night, O night! Alas, alas, alas, I fear my Thisby’s promise is forgot! And you, O wall, O sweet, O lovely wall, that stand between her father’s ground and mine! You wall, O wall, O sweet and lovely wall, show me your chink, to blink through with my eyne, my eyes!” Wall held up his fingers, and Pyramus went on: “Thanks, courteous wall; Jove shield you well for this! But what do I see? No Thisby do I see. O wicked wall, through whom I see no bliss! Cursed be your stones for thus deceiving me!”

Theseus said, “The wall, I think, since it has feelings, ought to curse back.”

Pyramus, dropping his verse, answered the Duke directly: “No, in truth, sir, he should not. ‘Deceiving me’ is Thisby’s cue: she is to come in now, and I am to spy her through the wall. You shall see, it will fall out exactly as I told you. Yonder she comes.”

Thisbe came in.

Thisbe said, “O wall, very often you have heard my moans, for parting my fair Pyramus and me! My cherry lips have often kissed your stones, your stones knit up in you with lime and hair.”

Pyramus said, “I see a voice! Now I will go to the chink, to spy if I can hear my Thisby’s face. Thisby!”

Thisbe said, “My love you are, my love, I think.”

Pyramus said, “Think what you will, I am your lover’s grace; and, like Limander, I am faithful still.”

Thisbe said, “And I like Helen, till the Fates kill me.”

Pyramus said, “Not Shafalus to Procrus was so true.”

Thisbe said, “As Shafalus to Procrus, I to you.”

Pyramus said, “O, kiss me through the hole of this vile wall!”

Thisbe said, “I kiss the wall’s hole, not your lips at all.”

Pyramus said, “Will you meet me at Ninny’s tomb straight away?”

Thisbe said, “Come life, come death, I come without delay.” Then Pyramus and Thisbe went out.

Wall said, “Thus have I, Wall, my part discharged so; and, being done, thus Wall away does go.” And he went out.

Theseus said, “Now the mural is down between the two neighbours.”

Demetrius said, “There’s no help for it, my lord, when walls are so willing to hear without warning.”

Hippolyta said, “This is the silliest stuff I ever heard.”

Theseus said, “The best in this kind are only shadows; and the worst are no worse, if imagination mends them.”

Hippolyta said, “Then it must be your imagination, and not theirs.”

Theseus said, “If we imagine no worse of them than they imagine of themselves, they may pass for excellent men. Here come two noble beasts in a man and a lion.”

Michael, the guide: The players mangle their famous lovers. “Limander” should be Leander, who swam the sea to reach his love Hero; “Helen” should be Hero, though Helen of Troy, who left her husband, makes an odd pledge of faithfulness; and “Shafalus and Procrus” are Cephalus and Procris, a married pair from Greek legend. “Ninny’s tomb” turns Ninus, a legendary king of Babylon, into a fool, “I see a voice” scrambles the senses, and the talk of the wall’s stones and hole carries a bawdy double meaning. When Pyramus stops to explain the cue to Theseus, it is plainly Bottom the player stepping out of the story; and the courtiers’ “mural” and “partition” both mean a wall, with “partition” also meaning a section of a learned speech. Earlier, when Quince said Pyramus “broached his boiling bloody breast,” “broached” meant stabbed open.

Lion and Moonshine came in.

Lion said, “You ladies, you whose gentle hearts fear the smallest monstrous mouse that creeps on the floor, may now perhaps both quake and tremble here, when a rough lion roars in wildest rage. Then know that I, one Snug the joiner, am a lion-fell, and no lion’s dam either; for if I came into this place as a lion, looking for a fight, my life would be in danger.”

Theseus said, “A very gentle beast, and with a good conscience.”

Demetrius said, “The very best at a beast, my lord, that I ever saw.”

Lysander said, “This lion is a real fox for his valour.”

Theseus said, “True; and a goose for his discretion.”

Demetrius said, “Not so, my lord; for his valour cannot carry his discretion, and the fox carries the goose.”

Theseus said, “His discretion, I am sure, cannot carry his valour, for the goose does not carry the fox. Very well; leave it to his discretion, and let us listen to the moon.”

Moonshine began, “This lantern presents the horned moon—”

Demetrius said, “He should have worn the horns on his head.”

Theseus said, “He is no crescent, and his horns are invisible within the circumference.”

Moonshine started again: “This lantern presents the horned moon; I myself seem to be the man in the moon.”

Theseus said, “This is the greatest mistake of all: the man should be put inside the lantern. How else is he the man in the moon?”

Demetrius said, “He doesn’t dare go in there because of the candle; for, you see, it is already in snuff.”

Hippolyta said, “I am weary of this moon. I wish he would change!”

Theseus said, “It appears, from his small light of discretion, that he is on the wane; but still, in courtesy, in all reason, we must wait out the time.”

Lysander said, “Proceed, Moon.”

Moonshine said, “All I have to say is to tell you that the lantern is the moon; I, the man in the moon; this thorn-bush, my thorn-bush; and this dog, my dog.”

Demetrius said, “Why, all these should be in the lantern, for all these are in the moon. But silence! Here comes Thisbe.”

Michael, the guide: Snug’s “lion-fell” can mean a lion’s skin, “fell” being an old word for hide, which fits the reassurance the rest of his speech gives: he is a man in a lion’s hide, and no lion’s dam, or lioness, either. It can also be heard as “a fierce lion,” in which case his words literally say the opposite of that reassurance; the text leaves the muddle as it stands. The fox-and-goose banter plays on a fox being cunning rather than brave, a goose being foolish, and foxes carrying off geese. Horns were the old joke-mark of a man whose wife was unfaithful; a candle “in snuff” needs its burnt wick trimmed, with a pun on being in a huff; and Hippolyta wishes this moon would change, as the real one does. In old folklore the man in the moon carried a bundle of thorns and kept a dog, which is why Moonshine brings both.

Thisbe came in.

Thisbe said, “This is old Ninny’s tomb. Where is my love?”

Lion roared, “Oh—” and Thisbe ran off.

Demetrius said, “Well roared, Lion.”

Theseus said, “Well run, Thisbe.”

Hippolyta said, “Well shone, Moon. Truly, the moon shines with a good grace.”

The Lion shook Thisbe’s mantle and went out.

Theseus said, “Well moused, Lion,” praising the shaken mantle as if the lion were a cat that had worried a mouse.

Demetrius said, “And then came Pyramus.”

Lysander said, “And so the lion vanished.”

Pyramus came in.

Pyramus said, “Sweet Moon, I thank you for your sunny beams; I thank you, Moon, for shining now so bright; for, by your gracious, golden, glittering gleams, I trust to catch sight of truest Thisby. But stay, O spite! But mark, poor knight, what dreadful grief is here! Eyes, do you see? How can it be? O dainty duck! O dear! Your mantle good, what, stained with blood! Approach, you Furies fell! O Fates, come, come, cut thread and thrum; quail, crush, conclude, and quell!”

Theseus said, “This display of grief, and the death of a dear friend, would nearly make a man look sad.”

Hippolyta said, “Curse my heart, but I pity the man.”

Pyramus said, “O why, Nature, did you make lions? Since vile lion has here deflowered my dear, who is—no, no—who was the fairest dame that lived, that loved, that liked, that looked with cheer. Come, tears, confound; out, sword, and wound the pap of Pyramus; yes, that left pap, where heart does hop.” He stabbed himself. “Thus die I, thus, thus, thus. Now am I dead, now am I fled; my soul is in the sky. Tongue, lose your light; Moon, take your flight!” Moonshine went out. “Now die, die, die, die, die.” And Pyramus died.

Demetrius said, “No die, but an ace, for him; for he is only one.”

Lysander said, “Less than an ace, man; for he is dead; he is nothing.”

Theseus said, “With the help of a surgeon he might still recover, and prove an ass.”

Hippolyta said, “How is it Moonshine has gone before Thisbe comes back and finds her lover?”

Theseus said, “She will find him by starlight. Here she comes; and her lament ends the play.”

Michael, the guide: Pyramus means the lion has “devoured” his dear, but says “deflowered,” robbed of her virginity. The Furies, called “fell,” or cruel, were avenging spirits, and the Fates were the three goddesses who spun, measured and cut each life’s thread; “thrum” is the loose end of a weaver’s thread, and a “pap” is a breast. “Quail, crush, conclude and quell” piles up four old verbs that all mean, roughly, overpower and destroy, and the “confound” in “Come, tears, confound” also meant destroy. Demetrius’s “die” is one of a pair of dice and the “ace” its single spot; Lysander tops him, and Theseus turns “ace” into “ass.”

Thisbe came back.

Hippolyta said, “I think she shouldn’t make a long lament over such a Pyramus. I hope she will be brief.”

Demetrius said, “A speck of dust would tip the scales as to which is the better, Pyramus or Thisbe: he as a man, God protect us; she as a woman, God bless us.”

Lysander said, “She has spotted him already with those sweet eyes.”

Demetrius said, “And thus she means, videlicet—”

Thisbe said, “Asleep, my love? What, dead, my dove? O Pyramus, arise! Speak, speak. Quite dumb? Dead, dead? A tomb must cover your sweet eyes. These lily lips, this cherry nose, these yellow cowslip cheeks, are gone, are gone: lovers, make moan! His eyes were green as leeks. O Sisters Three, come, come to me, with hands as pale as milk; lay them in gore, since you have shorn with shears his thread of silk. Tongue, not a word: come, trusty sword; come, blade, my breast imbrue!” She stabbed herself. “And farewell, friends; thus Thisby ends: adieu, adieu, adieu.” And Thisbe died.

Theseus said, “Moonshine and Lion are left to bury the dead.”

Demetrius said, “Yes, and Wall too.”

Bottom started up and said, “No, I assure you; the wall is down that parted their fathers. Would you like to see the epilogue, or to hear a Bergomask dance between two of our company?”

Theseus said, “No epilogue, please; your play needs no excuse. Never make excuses; for when the players are all dead, there is no one left to be blamed. Indeed, if the man who wrote it had played Pyramus and hanged himself in Thisbe’s garter, it would have been a fine tragedy; and so it is, truly, and very notably performed. But come, your Bergomask; let your epilogue alone.”

Then there was a dance.

Theseus said, “The iron tongue of midnight has counted twelve. Lovers, to bed; it is almost fairy time. I fear we shall oversleep the coming morning as much as we have stayed up late tonight. This plainly crude play has nicely cheated the heavy pace of the night. Sweet friends, to bed. For a fortnight we will keep up this festival, with nightly revels and new merriment.” And they all went out.

Michael, the guide: Demetrius’s “means” was an old word for laments, and “videlicet” is Latin for “namely.” Thisbe’s “Sisters Three” are the Fates again, whose shears cut each life’s thread, and “imbrue” means to drench in blood. A Bergomask was a rustic dance, named after Bergamo in Italy, and Bottom offers to let the court “hear” it, the senses muddled once more.

Puck came in.

Puck said: “Now the hungry lion roars, and the wolf howls at the moon, while the heavy ploughman snores, all worn out by his weary task. Now the burnt-down embers glow, while the screech-owl, screeching loud, puts the wretch who lies in woe in mind of a shroud. Now it is the time of night when the graves, all gaping wide, every one let out its sprite, to glide along the paths that lead to church. And we fairies, who run beside the triple Hecate’s team, away from the presence of the sun, following darkness like a dream, now are frolicking: not a mouse shall disturb this hallowed house. I am sent with broom before, to sweep the dust behind the door.”

Oberon and Titania came in with their train.

Oberon said, “Through the house give glimmering light, by the dead and drowsy fire; every elf and fairy sprite, hop as light as bird from briar; and this ditty, after me, sing, and dance it trippingly.”

Titania said, “First, rehearse your song by heart, to each word a warbling note: hand in hand, with fairy grace, we will sing, and bless this place.”

Then came a song and a dance.

Oberon said, “Now, until the break of day, through this house each fairy stray. To the best bride-bed go we, which by us shall blessed be; and the children there conceived ever shall be fortunate. So shall all the couples three ever true in loving be; and the blots of Nature’s hand shall not on their children stand: never mole, hare lip, nor scar, nor ill-omened mark, such as are despised at birth, shall upon their children be. With this field-dew made holy, every fairy go his way, and bless each separate chamber through this palace with sweet peace; it shall ever rest in safety, and its owner be blessed. Trip away; make no stay; meet me all by break of day.” Oberon, Titania and their train went out.

Michael, the guide: Hecate was a goddess of night and the moon, called “triple” because she was honoured in three forms; her “team” means the team drawing her chariot, not a group of companions, and the fairies picture themselves running alongside it, away from the sun and following the darkness. The text gives no words for the fairies’ song, only that they sing and dance. Oberon’s “couples three” are the three newly married pairs, and his promise that no mole, hare lip or birthmark will appear on their children rests on the old belief that such marks at birth were bad omens.

Left alone, Puck said: “If we shadows have offended, think but this, and all is mended: that you have only slumbered here while these visions did appear. And this weak and idle theme, yielding nothing but a dream, gentlefolk, do not condemn; if you pardon, we will mend. And, as I am an honest Puck, if we have unearned luck now to escape the serpent’s tongue, we will make amends ere long; else the Puck a liar call. So, good night unto you all. Give me your hands, if we be friends, and Robin shall restore amends.” And he went out.

Michael, the guide: Here Puck turns to the audience. “Shadows” means both spirits and actors, the word Theseus used for players earlier tonight; the “serpent’s tongue” is the hissing of a displeased audience; and “give me your hands” asks for applause. Robin is Puck’s other name, Robin Goodfellow.
