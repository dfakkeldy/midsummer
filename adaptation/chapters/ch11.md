# Chapter 11 — Bottom returns

In Athens, at Quince’s house, Quince, Flute, Snout and Starveling came in.

Quince said, “Have you sent to Bottom’s house? Has he come home yet?”

Starveling said, “There’s no word of him anywhere. No doubt about it, he’s been transported.”

Flute said, “If he doesn’t come, then the play is ruined. It can’t go ahead, can it?”

Quince said, “It isn’t possible. You haven’t a man in all Athens able to carry off the part of Pyramus except him.”

Flute said, “No; he simply has the best wits of any working craftsman in Athens.”

Quince said, “Yes, and the best figure too; and for a sweet voice he is a real paramour.”

Flute said, “You must say ‘paragon.’ A paramour is, God bless us, a wicked, worthless thing.”

Michael, the guide: Starveling’s “transported” can mean carried off, perhaps by spirits, or changed into another shape, and he doesn’t say which; both fit what the players saw at the rehearsal. Quince means “paragon,” a model of excellence, but says “paramour,” an illicit lover, which is why Flute corrects him so primly.

Snug came in and said, “Masters, the Duke is coming from the temple, and there are two or three more lords and ladies married as well. If our entertainment had gone ahead, we would all have been made men.”

Flute said, “Oh, sweet bully Bottom! So he has lost sixpence a day for the rest of his life. He couldn’t have missed getting sixpence a day: if the Duke hadn’t given him sixpence a day for playing Pyramus, I’ll be hanged. He would have deserved it. Sixpence a day for Pyramus, or nothing.”

Michael, the guide: “Made men” means men whose fortunes are made for good. “Bully” was a warm word for a fine fellow, and Flute imagines the Duke rewarding Bottom with sixpence a day as an allowance for life; it is Flute’s fancy, not anything the Duke has offered. Snug’s “two or three” lords and ladies is his own loose count.

Bottom came in and said, “Where are these lads? Where are these good hearts?”

Quince said, “Bottom! Oh, most courageous day! Oh, most happy hour!”

Bottom said, “Masters, I have wonders to tell you; but don’t ask me what, for if I tell you, I am no true Athenian. I will tell you everything, exactly as it happened.”

Quince said, “Let’s hear it, sweet Bottom.”

Bottom said, “Not a word out of me. All I will tell you is that the Duke has dined. Get your costumes together, good strings for your beards, new ribbons for your light shoes; meet at once at the palace; every man look over his part; for the short and the long of it is, our play has been put forward. In any case, let Thisby have clean linen; and don’t let the man who plays the lion cut his nails, for they shall hang out as the lion’s claws. And, most dear actors, eat no onions or garlic, for we are to breathe out sweet breath; and I have no doubt we’ll hear them say it is a sweet comedy. No more words. Away! Go, away!” And they all left.

Michael, the guide: Bottom contradicts himself in a breath: he won’t say what wonders, then promises to tell everything exactly as it happened, then refuses again, and he never does tell. Quince’s “courageous day” is an odd word for a happy one, perhaps another of his slips. False beards were tied on with strings, Thisby is Flute’s part, so the clean linen is for him in costume, and “preferred” means the play has been put forward or recommended; Bottom doesn’t say by whom or how he knows. And where Snug said the Duke was only now coming from the temple, Bottom says he has already dined; the text leaves that as it is.
