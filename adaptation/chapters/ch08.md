# Chapter 8 — Through the fog

Oberon said, “This is your carelessness. You are always making mistakes — or else you commit your knavish tricks on purpose.”

Puck said, “Believe me, king of shadows, it was a mistake. Didn’t you tell me I would know the man by the Athenian clothes he had on? And to this extent my work has proved blameless: it is an Athenian whose eyes I anointed. And to this extent I am glad it turned out as it did: I count this squabbling of theirs as good sport.”

Oberon said, “You see these lovers are looking for a place to fight. So hurry, Robin, and cloud over the night. Cover the starry sky at once with a drooping fog as black as Acheron, and lead these quarrelsome rivals so far astray that neither comes in the other’s path. Sometimes shape your tongue to sound like Lysander, and then goad Demetrius with bitter insults; and sometimes rant like Demetrius; and see that you lead them away from each other like that, until sleep, which counterfeits death, creeps over their brows with leaden legs and bat-like wings. Then crush this herb into Lysander’s eye. Its juice has this powerful property: by its strength it takes all error from his eyes and makes his eyeballs roll once more with their normal, accustomed sight. When they next wake, all this mockery will seem a dream and an empty vision, and the lovers will make their way back to Athens, joined in a league whose term will never end until death.

“While I employ you in this business, I’ll go to my queen and ask her for her Indian boy; and then I will release her charmed eye from the sight of the monster, and all things shall be at peace.”

Michael, the guide: Acheron is a river of the underworld in Greek myth, so Oberon wants a fog as black as the land of the dead. The “league” at the end of his speech is not a distance this time but an alliance, a bond that lasts until death. The “Indian boy” is the changeling Titania keeps, and the “monster” is Oberon’s word for Bottom with his ass’s head.

Puck said, “My fairy lord, this must be done in a hurry, for night’s swift dragons are cutting through the clouds at full speed, and over there shines the herald of Aurora. At its approach, ghosts wandering here and there troop home to their churchyards. Damned spirits, all those who have their burial at crossroads and in floods, have already gone back to their wormy beds; for fear that day should look upon their shames, they deliberately exile themselves from the light, and must keep company forever with black-browed night.”

Michael, the guide: Puck pictures Night as driven by swift dragons, racing to be gone before day. Aurora is the Roman goddess of the dawn, and her herald, or harbinger, is the morning star that rises ahead of her. Editors usually explain the spirits buried “in crossways and floods” as suicides, buried at crossroads, and the drowned, whose only grave is the water; it is Puck who calls them all damned, and Puck who says they hide from daylight out of shame.

Oberon said, “But we are spirits of a different kind. I have often made sport with the morning’s love, and like a forester may walk the groves even until the eastern gate, all fiery red, opening on Neptune with fair and blessed beams, turns his salt green waters into yellow gold. But all the same, hurry; make no delay. We may still get this business done before day.” And he left.

Michael, the guide: “The morning’s love” may mean Cephalus, the hunter loved by Aurora in Greek myth, or the dawn herself; editors read it both ways, and “made sport” could mean hunted or played. Neptune, the Roman god of the sea, stands for the sea itself: Oberon pictures sunrise in the east turning the green sea gold. Unlike Puck’s ghosts, he is not driven off by daylight, though he still urges haste.

Puck said, “Up and down, up and down, I will lead them up and down. I am feared in field and town. Goblin, lead them up and down! Here comes one.”

Lysander came back.

Lysander said, “Where are you, proud Demetrius? Speak now.”

Puck answered, speaking as if he were Demetrius, “Here, villain, sword drawn and ready. Where are you?”

Lysander said, “I’ll be with you right away.”

Puck said, still as Demetrius, “Follow me, then, to more level ground.” And Lysander went off, following the voice.

Demetrius came back.

Demetrius said, “Lysander! Speak again. You runaway, you coward, have you fled? Speak! Are you in some bush? Where are you hiding your head?”

Puck, now speaking as if he were Lysander, said, “You coward, are you bragging to the stars, telling the bushes you’re looking for a fight, and then you won’t come? Come on, you deserter; come, you child! I’ll whip you with a rod. Any man who draws a sword on you disgraces himself.”

Demetrius said, “So, are you there?”

Puck said, “Follow my voice. We’ll not put our manhood to the test here.” And they both went off.

Lysander came back.

Lysander said, “He goes ahead of me and keeps daring me on. When I get to where he’s calling, he’s gone. The villain is much lighter on his heels than I am. I followed fast, but he fled faster, so that I have fallen into a dark, uneven way, and here I’ll rest.” He lay down. “Come, gentle day! For if you once show me your grey light, I’ll find Demetrius and take revenge for this insult.” And he slept.

Puck and Demetrius came back.

Puck called, still in the voice Demetrius took for Lysander’s, “Ho, ho, ho! Coward, why don’t you come?”

Demetrius said, “Wait for me, if you dare; for I know well enough that you run ahead of me, changing your place every time, and don’t dare stand still or look me in the face. Where are you now?”

Puck said, “Come here. I am here.”

Demetrius said, “No, now you’re mocking me. You’ll pay dearly for this, if ever I see your face by daylight. Now go your way. Faintness forces me to stretch my length out on this cold bed. Expect a visit at daybreak.” And he lay down and slept.

Helena came back.

Helena said, “Oh weary night, oh long and tedious night, cut short your hours! Shine, comforts, from the east, so that I can go back to Athens by daylight, away from these people who detest my poor company. And sleep, which sometimes closes sorrow’s eye, steal me away for a while from my own company.” And she lay down and slept.

Puck said, “Still only three? Come, one more: two of each kind make up four. Here she comes, cross and sad. Cupid is a roguish lad, thus to drive poor females mad.”

Hermia came back.

Hermia said, “Never so weary, never so full of woe, soaked with the dew and torn by briars, I can crawl no further, go no further; my legs can’t keep pace with my wishes. Here I’ll rest until the break of day. Heavens protect Lysander, if they mean to fight!” And she lay down and slept.

Puck said, “On the ground, sleep sound. I’ll apply to your eye, gentle lover, remedy.” And he squeezed the juice on Lysander’s eyes.

Puck went on, “When you wake, you will take true delight in the sight of your former lady’s eye. And the country proverb everyone knows, that every man should have his own, in your waking shall be shown: Jack shall have Jill; nothing shall go ill; the man shall have his mare again, and all shall be well.” And he left.

Michael, the guide: Puck’s “two of each kind” means two men and two women, and he blames Cupid, the boyish Roman god of love, for driving the women mad. In his closing charm, “Jack” and “Jill” stand for any lad and his lass, and “the man shall have his mare again” is an old country saying that everything will go back to its rightful owner. He has put the remedy on Lysander’s eyes only; Demetrius has not been given it.
